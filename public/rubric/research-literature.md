# Empirical literature on assessment rubrics — for the 2026 MSc Graduation Project rubric

Compiled 2026-09-18. Context: the new TU Delft IDE rubric (in force 23 Sep 2026) is analytic,
8 learning objectives / 19 criterion descriptors / 4 performance levels with hidden hedonic
points (2/4/7/11), equal weighting, a published piecewise-linear points-to-grade formula,
independent double marking with reconciliation, and mandatory written justification for any
deviation from the computed grade. It replaces a 2018 rubric that was coarser (5 competencies,
12 sub-criteria), showed the grade in the column headers, had no stated aggregation rule
("final grade not necessarily the mean of the parts"), and was jointly assessed.

Each entry: claim — citation — what it implies for this rubric (support/challenge, stated honestly).

---

## 1. Analytic vs holistic rubrics: reliability, validity, design elements

**Jonsson, A., & Svingby, G. (2007). The use of scoring rubrics: Reliability, validity and
educational consequences. *Educational Research Review*, 2(2), 130–144.**
https://doi.org/10.1016/j.edurev.2007.05.002 · ERIC EJ796733
- Claim: Across 75 studies, reliable scoring is enhanced by rubrics that are analytic,
  topic-specific, and paired with exemplars/rater training — but rubrics do not automatically
  produce valid judgment; that requires a broader validity framework than the rubric itself.
- Implication: **Supports** the move to a fine-grained analytic rubric (19 descriptors vs 12) as
  a reliability lever, provided TU Delft also invests in exemplars/calibration — the rubric
  document alone won't guarantee validity, only reliability.

**Brookhart, S. M. (2018). Appropriate criteria: Key to effective rubrics. *Frontiers in
Education*, 3:22.** https://doi.org/10.3389/feduc.2018.00022
- Claim: Reviewing higher-ed rubric studies 2005–2017, Brookhart finds most studied rubrics are
  analytic, descriptive, 4–5 levels — and that a minority (7/51) degrade into rating-scale or
  counting language instead of describing quality, which weakens their use for learning (as
  opposed to grading).
- Implication: **Cautions** the new rubric's authors to check that all 19 descriptors describe
  *quality differences* at each of the 4 levels rather than collapsing into checklists/counts —
  a real risk when descriptors are compressed to fit a points table.

**Dawson, P. (2017). Assessment rubrics: Towards clearer and more replicable design, research
and practice. *Assessment & Evaluation in Higher Education*, 42(3), 347–360.**
https://doi.org/10.1080/02602938.2015.1111294 · ERIC EJ1129724
- Claim: Identifies 14 design elements that differentiate rubrics (e.g., quality levels,
  scoring strategy, weighting, exemplars, who developed it) and argues most rubric debates are
  confused because they compare rubrics that differ on several of these axes at once.
- Implication: **Directly useful as a design checklist** — the new rubric can be located
  precisely on Dawson's 14 dimensions (hidden points, equal weighting, published formula,
  4 levels, analytic) to make explicit design choices defensible individually rather than as a
  bundle.

**Sadler, D. R. (2009). Indeterminacy in the use of preset criteria for assessment and grading.
*Assessment & Evaluation in Higher Education*, 34(2), 159–179.**
https://doi.org/10.1080/02602930801956059 · ERIC EJ832788
- Claim: Breaking a holistic judgment into criteria increases apparent transparency/objectivity
  but does not capture the full complexity of multi-criterion qualitative judgment; Sadler
  documents six recurring "anomalies" in how assessors actually use preset criteria (e.g.
  criteria compliance without genuine merit, or genuine merit that doesn't fit the criteria) and
  concludes explicit grading models rest on weaker theoretical ground than usually assumed.
- Implication: **Genuinely challenges** the core mechanism of this rubric — a fixed,
  points-summed, formula-driven system is exactly the kind of "preset criteria" model Sadler
  shows breaks down at the edges. The rubric's own escape valve (written justification for
  deviation) is the appropriate response to this critique, not a refutation of it — see §6.

**Bloxham, S., Boyd, P., & Orr, S. (2011). Mark my words: The role of assessment criteria in
UK higher education grading practices. *Studies in Higher Education*, 36(6), 655–670.**
(see also Bloxham & related: "Bridging the gap between markers' tacit knowledge and students'
assessment literacy," *Compass*, journals.gre.ac.uk/index.php/compass/article/view/1224)
- Claim: Markers routinely rely on tacit disciplinary knowledge that exceeds and sometimes
  contradicts what written criteria say; moderation/calibration discussions surface this tacit
  language (phrases like "not clinically grounded") which can then feed back into rubric
  refinement.
- Implication: **Challenges** the assumption that 19 written descriptors will be applied
  uniformly by two independent supervisors — expect systematic gaps between what the rubric says
  and what supervisors actually reward; the reconciliation step is where this gap becomes visible
  and should be treated as a calibration opportunity, not just a disagreement to resolve.

## 2. Anchoring / grade visibility and score-first vs descriptor-first

**Malouff, J. M., Emmerton, A. J., & Schutte, N. S. (2013). The risk of a halo bias as a reason
to keep students anonymous during grading. *Teaching of Psychology*, 40(3), 233–237.**
https://doi.org/10.1177/0098628313487425 · ERIC EJ1014337
- Claim: Experimentally, 159 faculty who saw a strong oral presentation rated subsequent written
  work significantly higher than faculty who saw a weak presentation of the *same* written work —
  a halo effect driven by an irrelevant prior cue.
- Implication: **Supports (by analogy)** hiding the grade/points value during scoring: if a
  visible cue (like the old rubric's grade printed in the column header) can anchor judgment
  before criteria are weighed, hiding the final-grade mapping while scoring individual criteria
  — as the new rubric does with its "hidden points" — is a defensible bias-reduction move, though
  this study is about halo transfer between tasks, not within-rubric grade visibility per se, so
  the analogy is suggestive rather than directly tested.

**Anchoring bias in peer assessment: exploring the impact of recommendations on peer rating
value and peer feedback valence. *Assessment & Evaluation in Higher Education* (2026, online
first).** https://doi.org/10.1080/02602938.2026.2634708
- Claim: Raters shown a numeric anchor (recommendation/prior score) before rating produce scores
  and written feedback that track the anchor, even for identical underlying work.
- Implication: **Supports** removing the 2018 rubric's visible grade-per-cell design — a visible
  number is an anchor by this study's own mechanism — and supports scoring before consulting the
  formula's output, though no study found directly tests "score-first vs descriptor-first" as a
  named manipulation; treat this as inference from the general anchoring literature, not a
  rubric-specific finding. **Gap**: no direct empirical study was located comparing
  descriptor-first and score-first sequencing in rubric grading specifically — flag this as an
  assertion the rubric's designers should hold loosely.

## 3. Independent double marking vs joint/consensus marking

**Webster, F., Pepper, D., & Jenkins, A. (2000). Assessing the undergraduate dissertation.
*Assessment & Evaluation in Higher Education*, 25(1), 71–80.**
https://doi.org/10.1080/030750700116034
- Claim: Widely cited early study on UK dissertation marking showing markers' criteria weighting
  and process are often implicit rather than governed by a shared explicit procedure, raising
  reliability concerns absent a clear protocol for combining multiple criteria/indicators.
- Implication: **Supports** making both the criterion weighting (equal) and the aggregation
  formula (piecewise-linear, published) explicit rather than leaving it implicit, which is
  precisely the gap Webster et al. identify in unstructured dissertation marking.

**Bloxham, S., Hughes, C., & Adie, L. (2016). What's the point of moderation? A discussion of
the purposes achieved through contemporary moderation practices. *Assessment & Evaluation in
Higher Education*, 41(4), 638–653.** https://doi.org/10.1080/02602938.2015.1039932
- Claim: Reviews moderation practice (blind second marking, consensus/social moderation,
  expert moderation) and argues moderation's main value is building/checking *shared
  understanding* of standards, not mechanically improving reliability; also critiques that
  moderation can delay feedback, be shaped by power relations between senior/junior markers, and
  doesn't necessarily raise accuracy.
- Implication: **Partially challenges** the new rubric's independent-then-reconcile design: the
  reconciliation conversation is valuable for building shared standards (a real benefit), but
  Bloxham et al. would caution against assuming reconciliation mechanically improves the
  accuracy of the final grade, and flag the risk of a senior/junior supervisor power imbalance
  distorting reconciliation — worth an explicit process norm (e.g., who has to justify to whom).

**Haagsman, M. E., Snoek, B., Peeters, A. W. J., Scager, K., Prins, F. J., & van Zanten, M.
(2021). Examiners' use of rubric criteria for grading bachelor theses. *Assessment &
Evaluation in Higher Education*, 46(8), 1269–1284.**
https://doi.org/10.1080/02602938.2020.1864287 · ERIC EJ1320063
- Claim: Regression analysis of 318 graded theses against a 13-category rubric found criterion
  scores are unequally predictive of the final grade — "scientific quality," "professional
  attitude," and "structure" predicted overall grade far better than presentation/writing-style
  criteria — despite the rubric nominally treating criteria as comparable inputs.
- Implication: **Directly relevant caution** for equal weighting: even where a rubric doesn't
  formally weight, examiners' actual grading behavior implicitly weights some criteria more than
  others. Worth checking, once the new rubric is in use, whether the 8 learning objectives end up
  equally predictive of the computed grade or whether a de facto hierarchy emerges — this is an
  empirical question the new formula's transparency actually makes answerable, which is a genuine
  advantage over the 2018 rubric's opaque aggregation.

**Exploring the value of double marking in dissertation assessments: Classical Test Theory and
Item Response Theory approaches.** (ResearchGate record, publication venue not independently
confirmed in this search — verify before citing formally)
https://www.researchgate.net/publication/363939325
- Claim: Under Classical Test Theory, double markers of dissertations show poor raw agreement
  (threatening validity of a "true grade"); under Item Response Theory, the same markers show
  much closer agreement on students' underlying latent ability.
- Implication: **Nuances** the double-marking rationale — raw independent-score disagreement
  (which the new rubric's reconciliation step is designed to catch and force justification on)
  may look worse than the underlying judgment agreement really is; don't over-interpret score
  gaps between two independent supervisors as evidence of unreliable judgment without checking
  whether they're ranking students similarly.

## 4. Aggregation: formula vs holistic combination; equal weighting

**Sadler (2009), as above** — the core citation for "the whole is not the sum of the parts":
holistic, multi-criterion academic judgments resist decomposition into additive preset criteria
without loss, and assessors show systematic "anomalies" (e.g., adjusting the final grade to fit
an overall impression, or compensating one low criterion with judgment rather than the formula)
when a mechanical aggregation is imposed.
- Implication: **Challenges** the published piecewise-linear points-to-grade formula as the sole
  mechanism, which is exactly why the rubric's own "deviation must be justified in writing"
  clause matters — it is the designed release valve for Sadler's anomalies rather than a bolt-on;
  the literature suggests that valve will be used, and its use should be tracked/audited rather
  than treated as a rare exception.

**Haagsman et al. (2021), as above** — empirical evidence that even under equal nominal
weighting, criteria are not equally influential on the resulting grade in practice.
- Implication: **Supports monitoring, not abandoning,** equal weighting — equal weighting is
  defensible as a starting *policy* (it doesn't presume which objectives matter more before the
  fact), but Haagsman's finding means TU Delft should expect and check for emergent unequal
  influence once data accumulates, rather than assuming equal weighting produces equal impact.

**"Same grade for different reasons, different grades for the same reason?" *Assessment &
Evaluation in Higher Education* (2023).** https://doi.org/10.1080/02602938.2023.2203883
- Claim: Interview study with 10 examiners grading 3 undergraduate theses found that shared
  criteria and rubrics did not produce consistent grading — examiners reached the same grade via
  different criterion-weighting reasoning, and different grades from ostensibly the same
  reasoning; the authors warn that rubrics can create a false impression of assessment
  consistency.
- Implication: **Direct, honest challenge** to any claim that the new rubric's formula alone
  will produce consistent grades across the two independent supervisors — the paper's warning
  ("rubrics might give the impression assessment is more consistent than it is") is exactly the
  failure mode the reconciliation-plus-justification design needs to guard against; it argues for
  treating agreement on the *final number* as necessary but not sufficient evidence of shared
  standards.

## 5. Rubrics in design education: connoisseurship, tacit judgement, studio assessment

**Orr, S., & Bloxham, S. (2013). Making judgements about students making work: Lecturers'
assessment practices in art and design. *Arts and Humanities in Higher Education*, 12(2–3),
234–253.** https://doi.org/10.1177/1474022212467605
- Claim: In studio-based assessment, lecturers blend formal criteria with connoisseurship —
  tacit, experience-based recognition of quality — and factor in process, personality and
  development trajectory, not just the final artifact against criteria.
- Implication: **Challenges** a purely descriptor-driven scoring model for a design MSc
  graduation project, which (like studio work) is open-ended and process-dependent; supports
  retaining space in the rubric/process for supervisor judgment beyond the 19 descriptors — which
  the written-justification-for-deviation clause partially provides, but Orr & Bloxham would push
  further toward legitimizing tacit judgment openly rather than treating it only as a deviation
  to be justified against a "default" formula answer.

**Cowdroy, R., & de Graaff, E. (2005). Assessing highly-creative ability. *Assessment &
Evaluation in Higher Education*, 30(5), 507–518.** https://doi.org/10.1080/02602930500187113
- Claim: Highly creative ability resists the kind of transparent, criterion-referenced assessment
  used for conventional academic work, because creativity's value is often recognized only in
  retrospect and doesn't map cleanly onto preset performance levels.
- Implication: **Challenges** an assumption implicit in any fixed 4-level rubric — that
  originality/creativity in a design MSc project can be pre-specified at 4 quality tiers; this is
  a genuine tension for IDE's rubric to be honest about, not fully resolved by finer-grained
  descriptors.

**de la Harpe, B., Peterson, J. F., Frankham, N., Zehner, R., Neale, D., Musgrave, E., &
McDermott, R. (2009). Assessment focus in studio: What is most prominent in architecture, art
and design? *International Journal of Art & Design Education*, 28(1), 37–51.**
https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1476-8070.2009.01591.x · ERIC EJ833281
- Claim: Analysis of 118 studio-education articles across architecture/art/design finds
  published assessment practice emphasizes a broad set of indicators including process and the
  developing person, not only the final product against fixed criteria.
- Implication: **Supports** the new rubric's 8 learning objectives spanning process-oriented
  competencies (not just deliverable quality) if in fact they cover process/development — worth
  an explicit check that the 19 descriptors aren't skewed toward evaluating only the final report/
  prototype at the expense of process, which this literature says design educators themselves
  value.

## 6. Written justification / "recommended grade" systems

No study was found that directly evaluates a "computed-grade-plus-mandatory-written-deviation-
justification" system empirically (i.e., no RCT or comparative study of justification
requirements changing grading behavior or quality). The closest indirect evidence:

- **Sadler (2009)** — documents that assessors deviate from strict criteria application in
  practice ("anomalies"); a justification requirement is a reasonable institutional response to
  a documented behavior, but Sadler offers no evidence on whether *requiring* justification
  changes the deviation rate or quality of the resulting grade.
- **Bloxham, Hughes & Adie (2016)** — moderation literature suggests that making reasoning
  explicit (which written justification forces) is where shared standards get built, supporting
  the justification requirement as a *calibration mechanism* over time, not just a one-off
  accountability check.
- **Honest gap**: treat the written-justification clause as theoretically well-motivated by the
  broader indeterminacy/moderation literature, but note in the write-up for supervisors that its
  specific effectiveness (does it reduce unjustified grade drift? does it just produce
  boilerplate?) is untested and worth auditing empirically once TU Delft has a year of data —
  e.g., what fraction of gradings actually deviate, and do reconciled grades cluster near the
  computed value or systematically above/below it.

## Cross-cutting note for the supervisor-facing page

The literature is not univocal. Jonsson & Svingby and Haagsman et al. support the rubric's
core mechanics (analytic, fine-grained, transparent formula). Sadler, the 2023 "same grade for
different reasons" study, and the design-education literature (Orr & Bloxham, Cowdroy & de
Graaff, de la Harpe et al.) all push back on the idea that a formula-driven aggregation can fully
capture design-project quality — which is exactly why the rubric keeps a human deviation-with-
justification step rather than making the formula binding. The honest framing for supervisors is:
the rubric structures and makes comparable a judgment that the research literature says cannot be
fully mechanized; the formula is the default, the justification clause is where professional
judgment (including tacit, connoisseurship-type judgment) is meant to operate, and reconciliation
between two supervisors is a calibration exercise, not just error-correction.
