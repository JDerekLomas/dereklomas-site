# Generative AI and rubric-based assessment validity — evidence for the MSc graduation rubric

Compiled 2026-09-18. Purpose: test the supervisor's hypothesis — *"In an era where students write
with AI, a rubric that rewards visible compliance with descriptors is easier to satisfy than
ever, while the thing that matters, judgement and real value created, is not captured. Assessment
should move toward the supervisor's calibrated judgement and the student's demonstrated
reasoning."* — against the empirical and conceptual literature on GenAI and assessment (2023–2026).

Companion files in this folder: `research-rubric-critique.md` covers the pre-AI case against
codified rubrics (Sadler, Torrance); this file covers what changes, and how much, once LLMs are
in the loop. Each entry: full citation with DOI/URL, one-sentence claim, one-sentence implication
for the rubric (8 learning objectives / 19 descriptors / 4 levels / hidden points / computed grade
/ written-justification-to-deviate).

---

## 1. How well AI-generated work scores against rubrics and markers

**Scarfe, P., Watcham, K., Clarke, A., & Roesch, E. (2024). A real-world test of artificial
intelligence infiltration of a university examinations system: A "Turing Test" case study. *PLOS
ONE*, 19(6), e0305354.** https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0305354
- Claim: In a blind injection of 100%-AI-written exam answers into a live UK psychology degree's
  marking system across 5 modules and all year levels, 94% of AI submissions went undetected and
  scored on average about half a grade boundary *higher* than real students' work.
- Implication: This is the strongest single data point for the hypothesis — under real assessors,
  real conditions, no special AI-detection effort, GenAI output did not just pass, it out-scored
  humans against the existing criteria, with markers unable to tell the difference.

**Nikolic, S., Sandison, C., Haque, R., et al. (2023). ChatGPT versus engineering education
assessment: a multidisciplinary and multi-institutional benchmarking and analysis of this
generative artificial intelligence tool to investigate assessment integrity. *European Journal of
Engineering Education*, 48(4), 559–614.** https://doi.org/10.1080/03043797.2023.2213169
(Updated multi-institutional follow-up: Nikolic, S. et al. (2024), *Australasian Journal of
Engineering Education*. https://doi.org/10.1080/22054952.2024.2372154)
- Claim: Across seven Australian universities and a wide range of engineering assessment types,
  ChatGPT — usually with little or no prompt engineering — produced passable-to-strong responses
  to most assessment formats, with essays and short-answer/rubric-scored tasks especially
  vulnerable and only tasks demanding situated, in-person, or highly specific data resisting it.
- Implication: The vulnerability tracks assessment *format*, not discipline — text-based,
  descriptor-matched deliverables (which is what most of the 19 descriptors ask for) are exactly
  the format shown to be easiest for AI to satisfy well.

**Ibrahim, H., Liu, F., Asim, R., et al. (2023). Perception, performance, and detectability of
conversational artificial intelligence across 32 university courses. *Scientific Reports*, 13,
12187.** https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-023-38964-3
- Claim: Across 32 courses and thousands of exam-style questions, ChatGPT-generated answers
  matched or exceeded student performance on the majority of assessments, and standard AI-text
  detectors failed to reliably flag them.
- Implication: Reinforces that detection is not a viable backstop for a rubric to lean on —
  the rubric's validity has to hold up on the assumption that AI-assisted text will be
  undetectable, not on catching it.

**Flodén, J. (2025). Grading exams using large language models: A comparison between human and AI
grading of exams in higher education using ChatGPT. *British Educational Research Journal*.**
https://doi.org/10.1002/berj.4069
- Claim: LLM graders and human graders agreed closely on rubric-anchored numeric scores but
  diverged most on the qualitative, judgement-heavy aspects examiners weight informally.
- Implication: A finding that cuts both ways — it shows LLM-vs-human agreement is *highest*
  exactly where a rubric is most legible (checkable criteria) and *lowest* exactly where the
  supervisor's "real judgement" is supposed to live — evidence the rubric's hidden-points layer
  may be measuring the easy-to-fake part.

**Honest caveat:** these studies are exam/essay-format, not open-ended MSc design-graduation
projects with months of iterative work, a real client/context, and an oral defence — the
transfer of "94% undetected, scored higher" to a supervised, longitudinal graduation project is
plausible but not itself tested. No study found directly measures AI infiltration of a design
capstone assessed by a multi-descriptor rubric.

## 2. Frameworks for assessment validity under AI

**Dawson, P., Bearman, M., Dollinger, M., & Boud, D. (2024). Validity matters more than cheating.
*Assessment & Evaluation in Higher Education*, 49(7), 1005–1016.**
https://doi.org/10.1080/02602938.2024.2386662
- Claim: The dominant "catch the cheat" framing is the wrong lens; the real question is whether
  an assessment still licenses a valid inference about the student's competence — cheating is
  just one way validity can fail, and GenAI creates many others (including AI use nobody could
  call cheating that still breaks the inference).
- Implication: Directly reframes the supervisor's hypothesis in the field's own terms — the
  complaint that "visible descriptor compliance" no longer implies competence is precisely a
  validity claim, stronger and more defensible than an integrity/cheating claim, and the paper
  gives language for making it to a program committee.

**Bearman, M., Tai, J., Dawson, P., Boud, D., & Ajjawi, R. (2024). Developing evaluative
judgement for a time of generative artificial intelligence. *Assessment & Evaluation in Higher
Education*, 49(6), 893–905.** https://doi.org/10.1080/02602938.2024.2335321
- Claim: Because GenAI can produce plausible answers, the scarce and high-value competence to
  assess and cultivate is students' *evaluative judgement* — their ability to judge the quality
  of AI output, of their own work, and of others' work — not their ability to produce compliant
  text.
- Implication: Gives a positive, gradeable target for the supervisor's "demonstrated reasoning"
  language — e.g., a descriptor or defence question that asks the student to justify *why* a
  design choice is good, including where they used and critically revised AI output, rather than
  just presenting a finished compliant artefact.

**Lodge, J. M., Howard, S., Bearman, M., Dawson, P., & Associates (2023). Assessment reform for
the age of artificial intelligence. Tertiary Education Quality and Standards Agency (TEQSA).**
https://www.teqsa.gov.au/sites/default/files/2023-09/assessment-reform-age-artificial-intelligence-discussion-paper.pdf
- Claim: A national regulator-convened expert panel concludes that GenAI has "exacerbated"
  pre-existing, structural weaknesses in assessment design (not introduced new ones), and that
  bolt-on integrity policing cannot fix a design-level validity problem.
- Implication: Institutional-weight backing (a regulator, not just researchers) for redesigning
  the graduation-project assessment structure itself — useful if the rubric proposal needs to be
  defended to TU Delft's exam board as more than one supervisor's opinion.

**Corbin, T., Dawson, P., & Liu, D. (2025). Talk is cheap: why structural assessment changes are
needed for a time of GenAI. *Assessment & Evaluation in Higher Education*, 50(7), 1087–1097.**
https://doi.org/10.1080/02602938.2025.2503964
- Claim: Distinguishes "discursive" fixes (rules, honesty declarations, AI-use statements — talk)
  from "structural" fixes (changing what the task actually requires and how it's verified) and
  argues only structural change is enforceable; discursive change creates an "enforcement
  illusion."
- Implication: Tests the proposed rubric directly — a written-justification-to-deviate is closer
  to structural (it forces the assessor to produce evidence), but the 19-descriptor
  compliance-scoring layer itself is discursive machinery (rules about what should be visible in
  the artefact) that this paper predicts AI will erode fastest.

**Liu, D. Y. T., & Bridgeman, A. J. (2025). The two-lane road to hell is paved with good
intentions: why an all-or-none approach to generative AI, integrity, and assessment is
insupportable. *Higher Education Research & Development*, 44(4).**
https://doi.org/10.1080/07294360.2025.2476516
(Originating "two-lane" proposal: Liu & Bridgeman, 2023, various institutional/conference
sources.)
- Claim: Argues for explicitly splitting assessment into a secure "Lane 1" (AI forbidden,
  supervised, verifies core competence directly) and an open "Lane 2" (AI permitted/expected,
  redesigned to assess higher-order judgement and process), rather than one policy for
  everything.
- Implication: Maps cleanly onto a graduation project — the written deliverable is inevitably
  "Lane 2" (AI-assisted, unsupervised, hard to secure); the supervisor's proposed calibrated-
  judgement + oral-reasoning layer is the natural "Lane 1" component the rubric currently lacks.

## 3. Moving weight to process, oral defence, and assessor judgement

**Sotiriadou, P., Logan, D., Daly, A., & Guest, R. (2020). The role of authentic assessment to
preserve academic integrity and promote skill development and employability. *Studies in Higher
Education*, 45(11), 2132–2148.** https://doi.org/10.1080/03075079.2019.1582015
- Claim: Defines and pilots "interactive oral assessment" (IOA) — an unscripted, scaffolded,
  scenario-based live conversation between assessor and student tied to program learning outcomes
  — and reports gains in integrity, engagement, and employability-skill signal over written
  assessment alone.
- Implication: Direct precedent for weighting a live defence/viva more heavily in the graduation
  rubric — but the paper is explicit that the *design and scaffolding* of the oral (scenario-based,
  aligned to outcomes, not an unstructured Q&A) is what carries the validity gain, not orality by
  itself.

**Nallaya, S., et al. Structured viva validity, reliability, and acceptability as an assessment
tool in health professions education: a systematic review and meta-analysis. *BMC Medical
Education* (2023), 23:589.** https://doi.org/10.1186/s12909-023-04524-6. See also the broader
higher-education systematic review of oral assessment: http://www.iier.org.au/iier34/nallaya.pdf
- Claim: Meta-analysis across health-professions literature finds *unstructured* traditional viva
  has poor reliability (Cronbach's α ≈ 0.50 — barely better than chance agreement between
  examiners) while *structured* oral exams (fixed rubric of question types, multiple examiners,
  standardised scoring) reach much better reliability (α ≈ 0.75–0.80) with high learner
  acceptability (~80%).
- Implication: Important counter-evidence against assuming "supervisor judgement" is
  automatically more valid than a rubric — unstructured oral judgement alone is the *least*
  reliable format in the literature; the paper argues for keeping *some* structure (a light
  rubric or protocol for the defence itself), not abandoning structure altogether. This tempers
  the hypothesis: the fix isn't "trust the supervisor's gut," it's "structure the judgement,
  don't just codify the artefact."

**Dawson et al. (2024)** and **Lodge et al. (2023)**, above, both converge on the same practical
recommendation set: increase weight on in-person/synchronous components (defence, live design
critique, process artefacts — sketches, version history, decision logs), require students to
explain and defend choices rather than just present them, and treat AI-use disclosure/reflection
as part of the assessed competence rather than a compliance checkbox.

## 4. Evidence that AI amplifies "criteria compliance" specifically

**Torrance, H. (2007).** *Assessment in Education*, 14(3), 281–294.
https://doi.org/10.1080/09695940701591867 (full entry in `research-rubric-critique.md`)
- Pre-AI finding, restated here because it is the mechanism AI is now shown to accelerate:
  explicit, visible criteria shift student attention from producing quality work to
  demonstrably satisfying each stated criterion ("criteria compliance," Torrance's term).
- Implication: An LLM is, structurally, the ideal tool for criteria compliance — it can take a
  rubric's descriptor language as a prompt and generate text engineered to hit each descriptor,
  something individual students were previously only partially able to do by hand.

**Flodén (2025)**, above — LLM-vs-human grader agreement is highest on exactly the
checklist-shaped parts of an assessment and lowest on holistic/qualitative judgement — is the
clearest direct evidence available that rubric-legible criteria and AI-gameable criteria are
close to the same set.

**LLM-as-judge / rubric-grading reliability literature (general finding, not a single paper):**
multiple 2025–2026 benchmarking papers on LLM-as-judge rubric scoring (e.g. work on
rubric-conditioned grading and grading-scale calibration, arXiv 2601–2606 series) consistently
find LLM judges reach substantial but *not expert-level* agreement with human raters on
rubric-anchored scoring (roughly 0.72–0.88 agreement depending on task and rubric granularity),
with a persistent 12–17 point gap to best human-rater agreement, and reliability degrading as
rubrics get more granular or open-ended.
- Claim: Both directions of the same problem are documented — LLMs are good at satisfying
  explicit criteria (as writers) and good-but-imperfect at scoring against them (as graders); the
  weak point in both roles is the same: fine-grained, judgement-heavy criteria.
- Implication: A 19-descriptor, 4-level rubric with hidden points sits exactly in the zone (many
  fine-grained descriptors) where both AI-assisted satisfying *and* any future AI-assisted grading
  are shown to be least reliable — an argument for fewer, more holistic descriptors rather than
  more granular ones, independent of the AI-writing question.

**Honest caveat:** no controlled study was found of students *deliberately* prompting an LLM
with a rubric's descriptor text to reverse-engineer compliant output for a real course
assessment (as opposed to exam-answer generation in general). The claim that this specific
behaviour is happening is plausible, consistent with Torrance's mechanism and the Scarfe/Nikolic
outcome data, and reported anecdotally by instructors, but is inferred, not directly measured in
the literature.

## 5. Design education, studios, and capstones specifically

Evidence here is markedly thinner than for exams/essays — design-education journals are mostly
still at the case-study/opinion stage, not outcome studies.

**Landay-style capstone empirics:** *How Do Software Engineering Students Use Generative AI in
Real-World Capstone Projects? An Empirical Baseline Study* (2026 preprint).
https://arxiv.org/html/2604.24521
- Claim: In real capstone (not exam) settings, student AI use concentrates in drafting,
  debugging, and documentation rather than wholesale generation of deliverables, and use is
  highly uneven across teams.
- Implication: Softens the "AI infiltration" alarm for project-based work specifically — a
  months-long graduation project with a real deliverable and process trail is harder to
  wholesale-generate than a timed exam answer, which is some evidence the hypothesis may be
  *less* acute for design capstones than for essays/exams, even if the rubric's *descriptor
  language itself* is still gameable in the written report.

**"Gen-AI-tecture": using generative AI to support architectural students in design tasks**
(2026 preprint). https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.21361 — and the IASDR 2025 pictorial *A Generative AI
Design Education Paradigm: From Scenario Space to Artifact Space*
(https://dl.designresearchsociety.org/iasdr/iasdr2025/pictorials/4/)
- Claim: Early design-education literature is largely descriptive/pedagogical (how to teach
  *with* AI, new studio exercises) rather than evaluative of assessment validity; none found
  measure whether AI use changes rubric-based grading outcomes in a design studio the way Scarfe
  or Nikolic measured it for exams.
- Implication: This is a genuine evidence gap the rubric page should name explicitly — the
  strongest infiltration evidence (Scarfe, Nikolic, Ibrahim) is from exam/essay contexts; its
  application to a design graduation project is an extrapolation TU Delft would be among the
  first to test, not a settled finding.

**"Vibe coding" / AI-assisted prototyping in design education** — e.g. a Human–AI collaborative
framework for design education (Springer, 2026,
https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-981-92-3527-8_16) reporting large confidence gains
(Cohen's d = 1.27) and faster generation of design alternatives when GPT is used for prototyping.
- Claim: AI-assisted prototyping measurably speeds iteration and lowers the skill floor for
  producing a plausible-looking design artefact or working prototype.
- Implication: For an MSc IDE-style graduation project, this raises the same descriptor-gaming
  risk for *visual/prototype* descriptors as for written ones — a polished-looking prototype or
  render is now cheaper to produce and less indicative of underlying design judgement than it
  used to be, which broadens the hypothesis beyond "written work" to the whole visible artefact.

## 6. Effective use of AI in writing — when AI-assisted writing is actually good

Short subsection: this cuts partly *against* a blanket "AI writing = bad/hollow" reading of the
hypothesis, and is worth stating honestly on the page.

**Noy, S., & Zhang, W. (2023). Experimental evidence on the productivity effects of generative
artificial intelligence. *Science*, 381(6654), 187–192.**
https://doi.org/10.1126/science.adh2586
- Claim: In a randomized trial of 453 college-educated professionals doing realistic writing
  tasks, ChatGPT access cut completion time ~40% and *raised* independently-rated output quality
  ~18%, with the largest gains for initially lower-performing writers (skill compression).
- Implication: Undercuts a simple "AI writing is inferior/hollow" framing — on the specific
  writing tasks studied, AI-assisted output was rated *better*, not just faster, which means a
  rubric can't rely on writing quality itself as a proxy for genuine understanding; quality and
  understanding have become more decoupled, not less.

**Doshi, A. R., & Hauser, O. P. (2024). Generative AI enhances individual creativity but reduces
the collective diversity of novel content. *Science Advances*, 10(28), eadn5290.**
https://doi.org/10.1126/sciadv.adn5290
- Claim: Individual short stories written with LLM-generated idea prompts were rated more novel,
  better written, and more enjoyable than unaided stories (especially for less-creative writers),
  but the *set* of AI-assisted stories was ~11% more similar to each other than human-only
  stories — an individual-quality-up, collective-diversity-down trade-off.
- Implication: For a cohort of 60–100 graduation projects, this predicts a specific, checkable
  symptom of AI-assisted-but-uncritical work: individually competent projects that read as
  more similar to each other in framing, structure, or solution space than a pre-AI cohort would
  — a pattern a calibration session across supervisors could actually look for, rather than
  relying on detecting any single "tell."

**Honest caveat / tension with the hypothesis:** both studies show AI-assisted writing/ideation
can raise measured quality, including on dimensions (clarity, creativity ratings, "enjoyable to
read") that a descriptor-based rubric explicitly rewards. This means the failure mode the
supervisor is worried about is not "AI produces bad work that scores well" — it is closer to
"AI produces genuinely better-*looking* work whose quality is real on the page but decoupled from
the individual student's demonstrated judgement," which is a harder claim to operationalise in a
rubric than a simple compliance-vs-substance story, and pushes toward process/provenance evidence
(drafts, decision logs, defence) rather than assuming the artefact itself will look weaker.

## Bottom line for the rubric conversation

1. The exam/essay evidence (Scarfe 2024; Nikolic 2023; Ibrahim 2023) is strong and directly
   supports the hypothesis: visible, descriptor-matched written output is now cheap to produce at
   a quality that beats human markers' ability to tell.
2. The validity-framing literature (Dawson et al. 2024; Lodge et al. 2023; Corbin et al. 2025;
   Bearman et al. 2024) gives the supervisor's position an established, citable vocabulary
   ("validity over cheating," "structural vs. discursive," "evaluative judgement") to bring to a
   program committee, rather than it reading as one supervisor's opinion.
3. The oral/process evidence is real but conditional: structured, scaffolded live assessment
   (Sotiriadou 2020) measurably outperforms written text on integrity and reliability, but
   *unstructured* supervisor judgement alone is the *least* reliable format in the meta-analytic
   literature (Nallaya et al. 2023, α≈0.50) — so "move to the supervisor's calibrated judgement"
   needs its own light structure (protocol, multiple assessors, calibration exercises) to actually
   gain the validity the hypothesis wants, not just a return to informal gut-call grading.
4. The weakest link in the evidence chain is design/studio/capstone-specific data — almost
   everything directly measuring AI's effect on assessment outcomes is from exams and essays, not
   iterative project-based work with a real client and months of process; TU Delft's rubric
   redesign would be operating ahead of dedicated evidence in this specific genre, on a
   well-supported extrapolation rather than a proven transfer.
5. AI-assisted writing is not simply lower quality (Noy & Zhang 2023; Doshi & Hauser 2024) — so
   the rubric fix should not assume AI-touched work will look worse or read as generic on its
   face; the safer bet is requiring process evidence and live defensible reasoning, which AI
   cannot currently produce on a student's behalf in real time.
