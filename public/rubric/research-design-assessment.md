# Scholarship on assessing design work in design education

Compiled 2026-09-18 for evaluating the TU Delft IDE MSc graduation-project rubric (8 learning objectives / 19 descriptors / 4 levels / hidden points / computed grade), which replaces the 2018 rubric that assessed feasibility, desirability and viability of the *result* via visible grades and holistic team judgement.

Evidence status key: **[E]** = empirical study (data, coding, stats), **[C]** = conceptual/theoretical, **[P]** = polemical/advocacy, **[M]** = practitioner manual/guide.

---

## 1. Design judgement as the core competence

- **Nelson, H.G. & Stolterman, E. (2012 [2003]).** *The Design Way: Intentional Change in an Unpredictable World* (2nd ed.). MIT Press. ISBN 9780262526708. **[C]**
  Claim: Design competence is irreducible to method-following — it rests on roughly ten distinguishable types of *judgement* (default, deliberated, appreciative, appearance, navigational, etc.) that a designer exercises simultaneously, and "the ultimate particular" (the finished thing) can only be judged, not derived.
  Implication for the rubric: a 19-descriptor analytic instrument decomposes exactly what Nelson & Stolterman say resists decomposition — good rubric design should explicitly test whether *judgement* (holistic, particular-to-this-project) is still where the grade actually gets made, with descriptors as evidence prompts rather than independently addable point sources. See also the DRS paper directly applying their typology to design assessment: Deininger et al., "Design judgment: decision making in the 'real' world" (DRS Conference, https://dl.designresearchsociety.org/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=2733&context=drs-conference-papers). **[E/C]**

- **Schön, D.A. (1983).** *The Reflective Practitioner: How Professionals Think in Action.* Basic Books. / **Schön, D.A. (1987).** *Educating the Reflective Practitioner: Toward a New Design for Teaching and Learning in the Professions.* Jossey-Bass. ISBN 9781555420251. https://eric.ed.gov/?id=ED295518 **[C, grounded in observed studio dialogue]**
  Claim: Design ability is demonstrated through "reflection-in-action" — a real-time dialogue with the situation, best taught (and assessed) through a master coaching a student in a live studio review, not through propositional criteria. Schön draws his central case studies from an actual architecture design studio.
  Implication: this is the deepest scholarly root of "holistic team judgement" as an assessment form — it treats the *supervisory conversation itself* as where assessment validity lives, which cuts directly against pre-specified, independently-scored descriptors: a coach's real-time correction of reflection-in-action doesn't decompose into 19 checkable items.

- **Cross, N. (2006 [1982/2001]).** *Designerly Ways of Knowing.* Springer. Originally "Designerly ways of knowing," *Design Studies*, 3(4), 221–227 (1982), https://www.makinggood.ac.nz/media/1255/cross_1982_designerlywaysofknowing.pdf; and "Designerly Ways of Knowing: Design Discipline versus Design Science," *Design Issues*, 17(3), 2001. **[C]**
  Claim: Designers have a distinct cognitive/epistemic mode (solution-focused, abductive, using drawings as thinking tools) that is not reducible to scientific or purely analytic reasoning.
  Implication: if design cognition is genuinely "designerly" (solution-led, not criteria-led), an instrument built from decomposed *criteria* risks rewarding students who can retrofit justification to criteria rather than those who reason abductively — worth naming explicitly as a risk to check for in exemplar work.

- **Lawson, B. (2005/1980).** *How Designers Think: The Design Process Demystified* (4th ed.). Architectural Press/Routledge. **[E, protocol studies]**
  Claim: Empirical protocol studies (Lawson's classic architecture-vs-science student comparison) show designers work solution-first and iteratively test/evaluate emerging solutions against ill-defined criteria, rather than analysing the problem fully before proposing.
  Implication: supports the same caution as Cross — an analytic rubric applied only at the *end* (result) risks missing that quality was constructed through iterative solution-testing, so descriptors describing *process evidence* (not just outcome) matter for validity.

- **Dorst, K. (2008).** "Design research: a revolution-waiting-to-happen." *Design Studies*, 29(1), 4–11. DOI: 10.1016/j.destud.2007.12.001. **[C]**
  Claim: Design research/practice is at a paradigm crisis point (Kuhnian framing) because existing models can't account for the most valuable move in expert design — "frame creation," reframing the problem itself.
  Implication: a rubric of fixed learning objectives risks institutionalising the pre-paradigm model Dorst critiques (treating design as a bounded, decomposable problem-solving activity) unless a descriptor explicitly rewards reframing/frame-creation as a graduate-level competence.

- **Dorst, K. & Reymen, I.M.M.J. (2004).** "Levels of expertise in design education." In *Proceedings of the 2nd Engineering and Product Design Education Conference*, Delft, 2–3 Sept 2004. https://www.designsociety.org/download-publication/19588 **[C, model-building from literature]**
  Claim: Design ability develops through qualitatively different stages (novice → expert, extending Dreyfus/Dreyfus), and a graduating MSc student should be assessed against what is *developmentally reasonable* to expect at that stage, not a single fixed bar.
  Implication: directly relevant to a 4-level rubric — check whether the 4 levels map onto a defensible developmental model (e.g., novice/advanced-beginner/competent/proficient-toward-expert) or are just "poor/fair/good/excellent" restated, which Dorst & Reymen's framework would flag as under-theorised.

## 2. The crit / studio review as an assessment form

- **Dannels, D.P. (2005).** "Performing tribal rituals: A genre analysis of 'crits' in design studios." *Communication Education*, 54(2), 136–160. DOI: 10.1080/03634520500213165. **[E, discourse/genre analysis of real crits]**
  Claim: The crit is not one genre but (empirically) four distinct oral genres — descriptive, spatial-visual-focused genres each with different success criteria — and it functions as a ritual of disciplinary enculturation as much as an assessment event.
  Implication: warns that a single rubric applied uniformly across a "final assessment moment" may be scoring several genuinely different communicative acts (defending choices, narrating process, responding to critique) as if they were one performance.

- **Anthony, K.H. (1991).** *Design Juries on Trial: The Renaissance of the Design Studio.* Van Nostrand Reinhold. (20th anniv. ed. 2012, ISBN 9780974845012.) https://archive.org/details/designjuriesontr0000anth **[E, large-scale survey + interview study of architecture juries]**
  Claim: Based on a large empirical study, traditional design juries are frequently unreliable, biased by presentation skill, jury composition, fatigue and gender/power dynamics, and poorly aligned with what students actually learned.
  Implication: this is the strongest empirical case *against* pure holistic jury judgement (i.e., against the old 2018 model) — cite it honestly as the reason a more structured/documented process has appeal, while noting Anthony's own remedy was procedural reform of the jury, not replacing judgement with a scored checklist.

- **Percy, C. (2004).** "Critical absence versus critical engagement: Problematics of the crit in design learning and teaching." *Art, Design & Communication in Higher Education*, 2(3), 143–154. https://intellectdiscover.com/content/journals/10.1386/adch.2.3.143/0 **[E, qualitative]**
  Claim: The crit is powerful for disciplinary enculturation but is a poor vehicle for students to actually demonstrate understanding of the context of their own practice.
  Implication: supports having a *separate, structured* documentation channel (which hidden-points/descriptors can service) for the things a live crit systematically fails to surface — provided the descriptors target exactly those gaps rather than duplicating what the crit already does well.

- **Blair, B. (2006).** "'At the end of a huge crit in the summer, it was crap — I'd worked really hard but all she said was fine and I was gutted.'" *Art, Design & Communication in Higher Education*, 5(2), 83–95. https://www.researchgate.net/publication/233547387 **[E, interview study]**
  Claim: The learning value of crit feedback is highly dependent on students' emotional/self-perception state, and vague holistic comments ("fine") are frequently experienced as unusable, uninformative feedback despite being sincerely meant.
  Implication: direct empirical support for why *descriptors* (specific, worded criteria) can genuinely improve feedback quality over unstructured holistic commentary — a real point in favour of part of the 2026 redesign, distinct from the separate question of whether points should be summed into a computed grade.

- **Oh, Y., Ishizaki, S., Gross, M.D. & Do, E.Y.-L. (2013).** "A theoretical framework of design critiquing in architecture studios." *Design Studies*, 34(3), 302–325. DOI: 10.1016/j.destud.2012.08.004 (ScienceDirect: S0142694X12000592). **[C, synthesis of studio-critique literature into a framework]**
  Claim: Critiquing is under-theorised as a practice; the authors propose factors (critique goals, forms, foci, evaluative stance) that structure what actually happens when an expert critiques a student design.
  Implication: useful as a checklist for whether the new rubric's 8 learning objectives actually cover the *foci* experienced critics attend to (concept, process, craft, communication, context) or over-represent some at the expense of others.

- **Parnell, R. & Sara, R. (with Doidge, C. & Parsons, M.) (2007/2013).** *The Crit: An Architecture Student's Handbook* (2nd ed.). Routledge/Architectural Press. ISBN 9781138143517. **[M, practitioner guide grounded in >10 years of research]**
  Claim: Practical guidance for making crits constructive rather than intimidating, aimed at both students and tutors.
  Implication: lower evidentiary weight (a handbook, not a study) but useful for the practical "what makes a review session fair" checklist alongside Anthony's findings.

## 3. Assessment in art & design higher education

- **Orr, S. & Shreeve, A. (2017/2018).** *Art and Design Pedagogy in Higher Education: Knowledge, Values and Ambiguity in the Creative Curriculum.* Routledge. ISBN 9780367192501. **[C, drawing on the authors' own empirical studies]**
  Claim: Ambiguity is structurally central to creative education (not a defect to be engineered out), and assessment criteria in art/design HE routinely function as *post-hoc rationalisations* of judgements tutors have already made tacitly.
  Implication: the most directly relevant book-length source for this project — its central warning is that writing more explicit criteria does not, by itself, make grading more valid; it can just make tacit judgement harder to see and interrogate. Worth reading in full before finalising the rubric.

- **Orr, S. (2010).** "'We kind of try to merge our own experience with the objectivity of the criteria': The role of connoisseurship and tacit practice in undergraduate fine art assessment." *Art, Design & Communication in Higher Education*, 9(1), 5–19. **[E, interview-based]**
  Claim: Assessors describe deliberately blending "connoisseurship" (tacit, expert pattern recognition) with stated criteria, and are often more confident in the tacit judgement than the criteria used to justify it after the fact.
  Implication: for the hidden-points/computed-grade design specifically — this is the empirical grounding for the worry that a computed score can *launder* a tacit holistic judgement into false objectivity; the fix Orr's broader work points to is transparency about where connoisseurship still operates, not eliminating it.
  Orr's doctoral thesis (Univ. of the Arts London) frames assessment more broadly as a **social practice** — situated in local disciplinary culture and inflected by identity/power relations, not a neutral measurement act. Summarised in Orr & Shreeve above; see also https://shura.shu.ac.uk/5937/1/Orr_AAHE_SO_SB_Final_July.pdf.

- **de la Harpe, B., Peterson, J.F., Frankham, N., Zehner, R., Neale, D., Musgrave, E. & McDermott, R. (2009).** "Assessment focus in studio: What is most prominent in architecture, art and design?" *International Journal of Art & Design Education*, 28(1), 37–51. DOI: 10.1111/j.1476-8070.2009.01591.x. **[E, content analysis of published studio-assessment literature]**
  Claim: Effective studio assessment attends to four intertwined dimensions — **Product** (the artefact/content knowledge), **Process** (reflective/professional practice), **Person** (identity/growth as a practitioner), **Press** (environmental/contextual constraints) — and most published rubrics over-weight Product.
  Implication: a direct audit tool — map the 19 descriptors onto Product/Process/Person/Press and see whether Person and Press are represented at all; historically under-assessed dimensions per this study.

- **Cowdroy, R. & de Graaff, E. (2005).** "Assessing highly-creative ability." *Assessment & Evaluation in Higher Education*, 30(5), 507–518. DOI: 10.1080/02602930500187113. **[E/C, with a TU Delft co-author — de Graaff was at Delft]**
  Claim: Highly creative design ability progresses through three hierarchical cognitive stages — conceptualisation, schematisation, actualisation — each needing different (and separately assessable) memory/thinking skills, and conventional grading conflates them.
  Implication: a candidate theoretical basis for why 4 *levels* per descriptor could be developmentally meaningful rather than arbitrary — but only if the level descriptions actually track a cognitive-stage model like this one rather than a generic "poor→excellent" scale.

- **Davies, A. & Reid, A. (2000).** "Uncovering problematics in design education: learning and the design entity." Paper presented at *Re-inventing Design Education in the University*, Perth. https://ualresearchonline.arts.ac.uk/id/eprint/620/1/cltad_designeducation.pdf **[E, phenomenographic]**
  Claim: Students and teachers hold substantially different, often incompatible, conceptions of "what design is," and these variations (not just skill differences) drive divergent judgements of quality.
  Implication: before trusting inter-rater agreement on the new rubric, check whether supervisors share a conception of what graduate-level design competence *is* — disagreement may be conceptual, not just measurement noise the rubric can fix.

## 4. Rubrics in design studios / capstones (engineering-design empirical tradition)

- **Sobek, D.K. & Jain, V.K. (2004).** "The Engineering Problem-Solving Process: Good for Students?" and companion paper on design process/outcome instruments, ASEE Annual Conference. https://www.montana.edu/dsobek/career/documents/ASEE04_2425.pdf ; https://www.montana.edu/dsobek/career/documents/ASEE04_1331.pdf **[E, journal-coded process data + Client Satisfaction Questionnaire / Design Quality Rubric]**
  Claim: Coding actual student design-journal activity against a process taxonomy shows that *which* activities students spend time on (problem definition and concept generation vs. late-stage detailing) predicts client-rated design quality more than time-on-task overall.
  Implication: empirical support for descriptors that credit *process* (problem framing, concept exploration) as distinct from and predictive of *outcome* quality — relevant if the new rubric's 8 objectives split process from result.

- **Davis, D.C., Beyerlein, S.W., Trevisan, M.S., McCormack, J.** et al. — the **IDEALS** (Integrated Design Engineering Assessment and Learning System) programme, NSF Award #0919248, https://www.nsf.gov/awardsearch/showAward?AWD_ID=0919248. **[E, multi-year instrument-validation programme]**
  Claim: A capstone design-assessment framework built from three stakeholder perspectives (education researcher, student, practitioner) identifies four assessable performance areas — personal capacity, team process, solution requirements, solution assets — each with its own rubric, validated iteratively across cohorts.
  Implication: the most methodologically comparable prior art to what TU Delft IDE is attempting (multi-descriptor analytic rubric replacing single holistic grade in a capstone/graduation design project) — worth requesting the underlying IDEALS validation papers (Trevisan et al., *Journal of Engineering Education*) directly, since this literature is exactly the "does a decomposed rubric hold up empirically" precedent needed and wasn't fully surfaced by search.
  Related instrument: the **Engineering Design Process Portfolio Scoring Rubric (EDPPSR)**, reported with initial validity/reliability data via NSF Public Access (https://par.nsf.gov/biblio/10345718) — a directly comparable "portfolio + rubric" validation study worth pulling in full.

- General capstone-rubric literature (context, not individually load-bearing): reliability improves when rubrics are analytic, topic-specific and paired with rater training/exemplars, but a broader review found "no discernible pattern" that analytic rubrics are reliably better than holistic ones across contexts (see §7 below) — i.e., the "rubrics improve reliability" claim is real but conditional, not a given.

## 5. What "quality" in a design outcome means, and whether it's assessable

- **Vitruvius**, *De Architectura* (1st c. BCE), by way of Henry Wotton's 1624 translation "commodity, firmness, and delight" — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Firmness,_commodity,_and_delight. **[historical/foundational, not empirical]**
  Claim: The original design-quality triad — *firmitas* (structural soundness), *utilitas* (usefulness), *venustas* (beauty/delight) — has stood as the base description of "good design" for two millennia.
  Implication: useful framing device to show supervisors that desirability/feasibility/viability (2018 rubric) is a modern re-statement of an ancient triad, not an arbitrary Silicon Valley invention — and to ask what happened to *venustas* (delight/aesthetic quality) in the new 19-descriptor version, since it is the term most often silently dropped in engineering-flavoured reformulations.

- **Brown, T. (2009).** *Change by Design: How Design Thinking Transforms Organizations and Inspires Innovation.* Harper Business. **[P, practitioner/advocacy]**
  Claim: Good design solutions sit at the *intersection* of desirability (human), feasibility (technical), viability (business) — Brown/IDEO's popularisation of what by other accounts (Bardzell, Medium essays found in search) predates Brown at IDEO/Stanford d.school in less codified form.
  Implication: this is the direct ancestor of the outgoing 2018 rubric's three axes; it is polemical/consultancy literature, not empirical research — flag this explicitly to supervisors, since "desirability/feasibility/viability" is often cited as though it were a validated academic assessment model when its origin is a design-consultancy heuristic for *generating* ideas, not a tested instrument for *grading* finished graduate work. This mismatch (a generative heuristic repurposed as an evaluative rubric) is itself worth naming as a possible flaw in the 2018 scheme, separate from whatever flaws motivate the 2026 replacement.

- **Nelson, H.G. & Stolterman, E.** — "the good," "the true," "the ideal" (see §1). **[C]**
  Claim: Design's core evaluative question is normative — is this *good*? — and cannot be fully cashed out into instrumental criteria (does it work) without residue; "the good" in design is partly aesthetic/ethical judgement, not just fit-for-purpose.
  Implication: cautions against a computed grade implying that "good design" is a sum of independently scorable parts; Nelson & Stolterman would predict systematic underweighting of exactly the qualities (elegance, ethical stance, "rightness") hardest to write as a checkable descriptor.

- **Hekkert, P. (2006).** "Design aesthetics: principles of pleasure in design." *Psychology Science*, 48(2), 157–172. https://pure.tudelft.nl/ws/portalfiles/portal/54686438 — and Hekkert & Leder, "Unity-in-variety," DOI via *Acta Psychologica* (2016), S0001691815300858. **[E, TU Delft, experimental aesthetics]**
  Claim: Aesthetic appreciation of designed products is empirically modellable (unity-in-variety, most-advanced-yet-acceptable, maximum effect for minimum means) — it is not simply subjective/unassessable, though it is a distinct competence from usability or feasibility.
  Implication: directly TU Delft in-house evidence that aesthetic/formgiving quality *can* be described analytically to some degree — relevant if the new rubric has a descriptor for form/aesthetics, this is the citation to ground it, and a good check for whether the descriptor's wording matches these empirically-tested principles or is vaguer ("well-designed form").

## 6. TU Delft IDE's own tradition

- **Roozenburg, N.F.M. & Eekels, J. (1995).** *Product Design: Fundamentals and Methods.* Wiley. ISBN 9780471954651. Used at TU Delft IDE since 1991 (predecessor editions). **[M/C, the field's core Delft textbook]**
  Claim: Evaluation of a design proposal is an explicit, iterative step in the design process — comparing achieved results against a "list of requirements," with the evaluation cycle feeding back into both the proposal and the requirements themselves.
  Implication: this is the closest thing to an official Delft epistemology of "how do you judge a design" — worth checking the new rubric's descriptors against Roozenburg & Eekels' own vocabulary (requirements, criteria, evaluation cycle) for continuity/discontinuity with what students were taught methodologically earlier in the programme.

- **TU Delft OCW / Buijs, J. & Valkenburg, R. (2005).** *The Delft Innovation Method: A Design Thinker's Guide to Innovation.* Eleven International. ISBN 9789490947477. https://ocw.tudelft.nl/course-lectures/7c-delft-design-approach-context-delft-innovation-model/ **[M/C]**
  Claim: TU Delft's own process model (Delft Innovation Model) frames design as iterative divergent/convergent cycles across a corporate-innovation context, not a single linear pipeline.
  Implication: background context for supervisors rather than a direct assessment source; useful to confirm the rubric's process-related descriptors (if any) are compatible with the DIM's cyclical, not linear, model of how quality actually gets built.

- **Delft Design Guide** (2nd ed., "Delft Design Guide 2.0," TU Delft IDE, 2020). https://www.tudelft.nl/en/2020/io/february/hot-off-the-press-delft-design-guide-20 **[M]**
  Claim: Compiles Delft's own design "strategies, methods, models and views" as taught across the programme.
  Implication: cross-check that the rubric's 8 learning objectives use terminology consistent with what students were actually taught via this guide, to avoid an assessment/instruction vocabulary mismatch.

- **The 2018 rubric itself**: "Assessment form MSc Graduation project (July 2018)" and "Rubric MSc Graduation project (July 2018)," TU Delft IDE — referenced from the current graduation-manual document tree (https://filelist.tudelft.nl/Studentenportal/Faculteitspecifiek/IO/Onderwijs/graduation/Downloads/30%20EC%20Downloads/Graduation%20Manual_January%202023%20(1).pdf and https://repository.tudelft.nl/file/File_2d7b28a2-f652-4f5b-8a86-086db3310d9d). **[institutional document, not peer-reviewed research]**
  Note: I was not able to find a peer-reviewed publication *by TU Delft IDE staff* specifically validating or critiquing either the 2018 or 2026 rubric (no hits for "graduation project assessment Industrial Design Engineering Delft" turning up a journal article). This appears to be an internal-document gap, not a literature gap — worth asking IDE's own education-quality office (or the Teaching Support / "Design a Rubric" guide at https://www.tudelft.nl/en/teaching-support/educational-advice/assess/projects-and-assignments/design-rubric) whether any internal evaluation report exists, since it would not surface in academic search.

## 7. Empirical comparisons: holistic expert judgement vs. analytic rubric

Honest summary: **direct head-to-head studies specifically on design-studio or graduation-project work (vs. later real-world outcomes like awards, patents, or market success) are scarce or absent in what surfaced.** The clearest empirical evidence found is adjacent, not on-target:

- **Anthony (1991)**, above — empirical case that unstructured holistic jury judgement in design education is unreliable and biased (§2). **[E]**
- **Yeo, S.W., Signorelli, C., Vo, K. & Smith, G. (2024).** "A Retrospective Cohort Analysis Comparing Analytic and Holistic Marking Rubrics in Medical Research Education." DOI: 10.1177/23821205241277337, PMC11359436. **[E]** — not design education, but a directly relevant methodological comparison: analytic rubrics produced lower inter-marker discrepancy and stronger correlation between assessors (r = 0.36) than holistic marking (r = 0.24) on written research work.
- Broader rubric-research reviews (e.g., Frontiers in Education, 2018, "Appropriate Criteria: Key to Effective Rubrics," DOI: 10.3389/feduc.2018.00022) report **no consistent pattern** that analytic rubrics are more reliable or valid than holistic judgement across contexts — reliability gains depend heavily on rater training, exemplars, and criteria specificity, not the analytic/holistic choice per se. **[E, review]**
- No study was found that validates rubric-based (or holistic-jury-based) design-education grades against *later* real-world outcomes (patents, market success, design awards) — this appears to be a genuine gap in the literature, not a search failure; it's frequently noted as an open problem in the design-expertise literature (Dorst 2008; Cowdroy & de Graaff 2005) rather than answered.

**Bottom line for the rubric-evaluation page**: the honest claim to make is "structured/analytic assessment reliably reduces *inter-rater disagreement* on discrete criteria (moderate empirical support, mostly from outside design education), but there is no direct evidence it improves *validity* — i.e., that it better predicts which graduation projects were actually good design work — and design-education's own foundational literature (Nelson & Stolterman, Schön, Orr) argues on principle that some portion of design quality is a matter of expert judgement that resists full decomposition." This is a defensible, non-polemical position to put in front of supervisors: it neither dismisses the new rubric as scientism nor pretends it is empirically proven superior to the 2018 approach.

## Suggested framing for the supervisor-facing page

1. Lead with Orr & Shreeve + Nelson & Stolterman: ambiguity and judgement are not bugs to engineer away; ask supervisors to treat descriptors as *prompts for evidence*, not independently-summable point sources.
2. Use de la Harpe et al.'s Product/Process/Person/Press to audit coverage of the 19 descriptors.
3. Use Anthony (1991) honestly as the empirical case *for* moving away from pure holistic jury judgement — the 2018 rubric's own history of jury unreliability is real, not invented.
4. Use Blair (2006) and Percy (2004) to justify *why* explicit descriptors can genuinely improve feedback quality, separate from the grading-computation question.
5. Flag the Brown/IDEO desirability-feasibility-viability triad as a generative heuristic repurposed for grading, not a validated assessment instrument — this is a real weakness of the *outgoing* 2018 rubric worth naming.
6. Be explicit with supervisors that no study directly validates either the old or the new rubric against real-world design-quality outcomes; frame the 2026 rubric as a reasoned bet informed by adjacent literature, not an empirically proven improvement.
