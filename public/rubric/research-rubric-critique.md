# Scholarly critique of assessment rubrics — testing the supervisor's position

Compiled 2026-09-18. Purpose: stress-test one specific claim a TU Delft design supervisor might
raise against the new MSc graduation-project rubric: *"Evaluation of a design project is
inherently subjective. A rubric that hides that behind points and a formula is less transparent,
not more. What is needed is written justification of the judgement and alignment between
assessors on what quality looks like."*

This file is organized around that claim, not around rubrics-in-general (see
`research-literature.md` in this folder for the broader design-element literature). Each entry:
one-sentence claim, full citation, one-sentence implication for the rubric conversation. Honest
counterargument (Panadero & Jonsson) is included in full, not just cited.

---

## 1. Sadler: criteria and standards cannot be fully codified

**Sadler, D. R. (2014). The futility of attempting to codify academic achievement standards.
*Higher Education*, 67(3), 273–288.** https://doi.org/10.1007/s10734-013-9649-1
- Claim: Written descriptions of achievement standards (rubric levels, grade descriptors) can
  never be made complete or self-sufficient because qualitative judgment about complex work
  requires tacit knowledge that resists full verbal specification — codification projects
  therefore chase a target that recedes as detail is added.
- Implication: **Directly supports** the supervisor's premise — a rubric's descriptor language,
  however detailed, cannot substitute for the assessor's tacit sense of quality; it can only
  gesture toward it.

**Sadler, D. R. (2009). Transforming holistic assessment and grading into a vehicle for complex
learning. In G. Joughin (Ed.), *Assessment, Learning and Judgement in Higher Education*
(pp. 45–63). Springer, Dordrecht.** https://doi.org/10.1007/978-1-4020-8905-3_4
- Claim: Holistic judgment of complex work is a legitimate, learnable expert skill — developed
  through calibration: assessors deliberately comparing judgments against each other and against
  exemplars, not through decomposing the judgment into scored sub-criteria.
- Implication: **Supplies the alternative mechanism** the supervisor is gesturing at —
  "alignment between assessors on what quality looks like" is precisely Sadler's calibration,
  and it targets assessors' shared understanding, not the rubric document.

**Sadler, D. R. (2009). Indeterminacy in the use of preset criteria for assessment and grading.
*Assessment & Evaluation in Higher Education*, 34(2), 159–179.**
https://doi.org/10.1080/02602930801956059
- Claim: In practice, assessors routinely encounter cases where "criteria compliance" and
  "genuine quality" diverge — students who tick every criterion box without producing good work,
  and good work that doesn't map cleanly onto the criteria — which Sadler documents as recurring,
  irreducible anomalies rather than rare edge cases.
- Implication: **Predicts specific failure modes** a points-based rubric will hit in design
  projects (a technically complete but uninspired project scoring well; a genuinely original
  project that doesn't fit the descriptor language) — useful as concrete discussion prompts with
  supervisors, not just an abstract warning.

## 2. Torrance: explicit criteria produce "criteria compliance," not learning

**Torrance, H. (2007). Assessment as learning? How the use of explicit learning objectives,
assessment criteria and feedback in post-secondary education and training can come to dominate
learning. *Assessment in Education: Principles, Policy & Practice*, 14(3), 281–294.**
https://doi.org/10.1080/09695940701591867
- Claim: Making criteria maximally explicit and transparent (to satisfy fairness/accountability
  demands) tends to shift student and sometimes assessor attention from the underlying quality of
  work toward demonstrably satisfying each stated criterion — Torrance calls the endpoint
  "criteria compliance" replacing "learning."
- Implication: **Sharpens the supervisor's transparency point into a mechanism** — a formula that
  rewards checking boxes can produce students (and graders) who optimize for the rubric's letter
  rather than the project's substance, which is a real cost of "more transparency," not just a
  loss of nuance.

**Torrance, H. (2012). Formative assessment at the crossroads: Conformative, deformative and
transformative assessment. *Oxford Review of Education*, 38(3), 323–342.**
https://doi.org/10.1080/03054985.2012.689693
- Claim: Extends the 2007 argument — assessment systems built around explicit, compliance-checkable
  criteria tend toward "conformative" assessment (students converge on what's rewarded) rather
  than genuinely transformative learning, regardless of good intentions in the criteria's design.
- Implication: **Reinforces** that the risk is systemic (a property of how criteria-plus-points
  systems function), not a matter of writing better descriptor language.

## 3. Bloxham et al.: assessors don't actually apply criteria consistently

**Bloxham, S., den-Outer, B., Hudson, J., & Price, M. (2016). Let's stop the pretence of
consistent marking: exploring the multiple limitations of assessment criteria.
*Assessment & Evaluation in Higher Education*, 41(3), 466–481.**
https://doi.org/10.1080/02602938.2015.1024607
- Claim: Using repertory-grid interviews with 24 experienced assessors across four disciplines,
  the authors show marking judgments are complex, intuitive, and tacit — assessors vary in which
  criteria they notice, how they rank them, and how they score them — and conclude that "using
  explicit assessment criteria...cannot achieve full transparency, consistency and fairness."
- Implication: **Direct empirical support** for the supervisor's core claim — a rubric's apparent
  precision (points, weighted formula) papers over marker variability that the study shows is
  real and not fixable by writing sharper criteria; social moderation (assessors talking to each
  other) helped more than better wording.

**Bloxham, S., & Boyd, P. (2012). Accountability in grading student work: securing academic
standards in a twenty-first century quality assurance context. *British Educational Research
Journal*, 38(4), 615–634.** https://doi.org/10.1080/01411926.2011.569007
- Claim: The push for explicit, auditable grading criteria is driven by institutional
  accountability and quality-assurance pressures more than by evidence that it improves standards
  — and it can crowd out the professional, dialogic practices (moderation, discussion of
  exemplars) that actually build assessor agreement.
- Implication: **Names the institutional trap** — a formula-driven rubric is attractive to a
  faculty because it is auditable and defensible to external reviewers (NVAO), not necessarily
  because it produces better or fairer judgments; worth naming explicitly so the choice is made
  consciously rather than by QA default.

## 4. Bearman & Ajjawi: rubrics-as-transparency is the wrong metaphor

**Bearman, M., & Ajjawi, R. (2021). Can a rubric do more than be transparent? Invitation as a new
metaphor for assessment criteria. *Studies in Higher Education*, 46(2), 359–368.**
https://doi.org/10.1080/03075079.2019.1637842
- Claim: The "transparency" metaphor underlying most rubric design assumes assessment standards
  are fixed, fully explicable things a rubric merely reveals — but quality in complex work is
  actually enacted and co-constructed in the specific task, so an "invitational" rubric (that
  opens a productive space for judgment) fits better than a rubric that pretends to fully specify
  standards in advance.
- Implication: **Gives vocabulary for the supervisor's alternative** — reframe the rubric's job
  from "make the hidden formula visible" to "invite consistent professional judgment," which
  points toward written justification and calibration discussions rather than tighter points
  tables.

## 5. The rubrics-in-general debate, including the strongest defense

**Popham, W. J. (1997). What's wrong — and what's right — with rubrics. *Educational
Leadership*, 55(2), 72–75.** https://www.ascd.org/el/articles/whats-wrong-and-whats-right-with-rubrics
- Claim: Most classroom rubrics fail not because rubrics are a bad idea but because they are
  written badly — too task-specific, or so general they merge the skill being assessed with
  surface features of the artifact (Popham calls this conflating "the skill" with "the task").
- Implication: A **moderate** critique — useful for arguing the *engineering* of the IDE rubric's
  19 descriptors matters (do they describe design-thinking quality, or just checklist items?)
  rather than for arguing rubrics as a genre are the problem.

**Kohn, A. (2006). The trouble with rubrics. *English Journal*, 95(4), 12–15.**
https://www.alfiekohn.org/article/trouble-rubrics/
- Claim: Kohn argues rubrics (especially numerically scored ones) function mainly to make grading
  faster and more defensible to administrators, at the cost of reducing complex, holistic work to
  a checklist and training students to write/design for the rubric rather than for the work's own
  sake.
- Implication: The **strongest rhetorical version** of the supervisor's worry — worth citing as
  the position being guarded against, while noting (below) that Panadero & Jonsson find Kohn's
  claims largely unsupported by direct evidence.

**Wilson, M. (2006). *Rethinking Rubrics in Writing Assessment*. Heinemann.**
- Claim: Book-length argument that analytic rubrics in writing assessment tend to reward
  formulaic, rubric-legible prose over genuine voice and risk-taking, because the traits that are
  easiest to specify and score are not the traits that make writing good.
- Implication: **Analogous risk for design projects** — the criteria easiest to write down and
  score (documentation completeness, process steps followed) are not necessarily what makes a
  graduation project excellent; the rubric should be checked for this bias.

**Panadero, E., & Jonsson, A. (2020). A critical review of the arguments against the use of
rubrics. *Educational Research Review*, 30, 100329.**
https://doi.org/10.1016/j.edurev.2020.100329
- Claim: Reviewing 27 critical publications and 93 extracted claims (Popham, Kohn, Wilson and
  others among them) across six criticism themes, the authors find most criticisms rest on
  anecdote or theoretical assertion rather than direct empirical evidence, several rely on
  studies of poorly-designed rubrics rather than rubrics per se, and a few misread the studies
  they cite.
- Implication: **This is the honest counterweight.** It does not rebut Sadler, Torrance, or
  Bloxham et al. (who bring their own primary data and are treated by Panadero & Jonsson as more
  credible than the anecdotal critics) — but it should stop the case against rubrics from leaning
  on Kohn/Popham-style rhetorical claims as if they were established findings. The rubric
  conversation should cite Sadler/Torrance/Bloxham as the evidence base, and treat Kohn as
  illustrative rather than probative.

## 6. Shay, Yorke, Knight: summative grading of complex work rests on shaky ground generally

**Shay, S. (2005). The assessment of complex tasks: a double reading. *Studies in Higher
Education*, 30(6), 663–679.** https://doi.org/10.1080/03075070500339988
- Claim: Assessors of complex, authentic tasks (Shay studies design/professional assessment)
  actually perform a "double reading" — matching the work against stated criteria, and separately
  reading it holistically as a professional/social act situated in a discipline — and it is the
  second, tacit reading that resolves most real disagreements, not the criteria.
- Implication: **Empirically grounds** exactly the kind of task at stake here (design projects) —
  criteria-matching is necessary but assessors' actual verdicts come from a second, contextual
  judgment the criteria don't capture, which is the process a written-justification requirement
  makes visible.

**Yorke, M. (2011). Summative assessment: dealing with the 'measurement fallacy'. *Studies in
Higher Education*, 36(3), 251–273.** https://doi.org/10.1080/03075070903545082
- Claim: Grading practices implicitly borrow the language and apparent precision of scientific
  measurement (points, decimals, weighted averages) even though grading complex academic work
  does not meet the conditions that make measurement meaningful — Yorke calls this the
  "measurement fallacy."
- Implication: **Names the formula's core risk directly** — a piecewise-linear points-to-grade
  formula produces a number with spurious precision; the fix Yorke proposes is treating grades as
  professional judgments reported with their reasoning, not as measurements.

**Knight, P. T. (2002). Summative assessment in higher education: practices in disarray.
*Studies in Higher Education*, 27(3), 275–286.** https://doi.org/10.1080/03075070220000662
- Claim: Surveying UK summative assessment practice broadly, Knight argues high-stakes grading of
  complex work is in such disarray (differing purposes, poor reliability, contested meanings of
  grades) that treating grades as reliable, comparable measures is risky, and the deeper fix is
  reappraising what assessment is *for*, not just tightening technique.
- Implication: A **structural warning** — better rubric engineering (Knight's "failure of
  technique" framing) can improve consistency at the margins but cannot resolve the underlying
  disagreement about what a graduation grade is supposed to represent; worth flagging so the
  rubric isn't oversold as a fix for that deeper question.

## 7. Evidence that points-based rubrics compress/distort scores

**Rezaei, A. R., & Lovorn, M. (2010). Reliability and validity of rubrics for assessment through
writing. *Assessing Writing*, 15(1), 18–39.** https://doi.org/10.1016/j.asw.2010.01.003
- Claim: In a controlled study, trained raters using a detailed rubric were still significantly
  swayed by surface mechanics (spelling, grammar, length) over substantive content quality, and
  untrained/inexperienced use of a rubric did not reliably improve validity — the rubric's
  presence did not neutralize rater bias.
- Implication: **Cautions against assuming the rubric itself does the calibration work** — without
  rater training and discussion, a detailed points rubric can still produce biased, clustered
  scores; this argues for investing effort in assessor calibration sessions, not just in rubric
  wording.
- Note: general central-tendency/compression effects (raters avoiding extreme scores, clustering
  near the middle of a scale) are a well-documented psychometric phenomenon in rating-scale
  research broadly; Rezaei & Lovorn is the most directly citable rubric-specific study located in
  this search. If a harder claim about grade-distribution compression specifically for the new
  IDE rubric is needed, that would require an empirical rerun/analysis of TU Delft's own grade
  data pre/post rubric change — flagged as a gap, not fabricated.

## 8. The alternative the supervisor is proposing: written justification + calibration

**Sadler (2009, cited in §1)** is the primary source for calibration as an alternative to
codification: assessors compare judgments against each other and against benchmark cases until
their independent verdicts converge, and this convergence — not the wording of criteria — is what
produces genuine (not merely apparent) consistency.

**Shay (2005, cited in §6)** and **Bloxham et al. (2016, cited in §3)** both provide empirical
support that the "second reading" or tacit professional judgment assessors already perform is
where real agreement or disagreement gets resolved — a written-justification requirement makes
this reasoning visible and reviewable (by a second assessor, by the Board of Examiners, by NVAO)
in a way a hidden points formula does not.

**Bearman & Ajjawi (2021, cited in §4)** supply the reframe: transparency is better served by
assessors being able to *articulate why* something is good work than by a rubric that claims to
have specified in advance what good work is.

Taken together, these give the supervisor's proposed alternative — narrative justification of the
grade plus deliberate calibration among assessors (shared exemplars, moderation meetings,
reconciliation discussions after independent marking) — a credible evidence base, distinct from
simply asserting rubrics are bad.

## Bottom line for the rubric conversation

The supervisor's position is well supported by primary empirical and theoretical literature
(Sadler, Torrance, Bloxham et al., Shay, Yorke, Knight) — not just by the more rhetorical
anti-rubric essays (Popham, Kohn) that Panadero & Jonsson (2020) show are weakly evidenced. The
honest synthesis is: a rubric's numeric/formula apparatus does not eliminate the underlying
subjectivity of judging a design project, and can create a false sense of objectivity that is
*worse* than acknowledged subjectivity (Torrance's criteria-compliance risk; Yorke's measurement
fallacy). The literature converges on a specific alternative, not "no rubric": explicit written
justification of the grade, and social calibration/moderation of assessors on shared examples of
quality — mechanisms the new rubric already gestures at (mandatory written justification for
deviation) and could strengthen further (structured calibration sessions, exemplar cases) rather
than relying on the points formula to do the work of producing agreement.

The one area not covered by this literature — and worth being honest about — is whether the
*specific* new IDE rubric's design (hidden points, 4-level analytic descriptors, published
formula) performs worse or better than the 2018 rubric it replaces. That's an empirical question
about TU Delft's own grade data, not something the general literature can answer; treat this
review as informing the *design conversation*, not as a verdict on this particular rubric's
outcomes.
