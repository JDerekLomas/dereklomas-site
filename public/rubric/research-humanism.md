# The humanist critique of metric-driven assessment — research notes

Prepared to support a TU Delft MSc graduation-project rubric that keeps evaluation as a
reasoned, calibrated expert judgement rather than a hidden formula. Six sections below,
each with sources (author, year, work, URL where found), a one-sentence claim, and a
one-sentence implication for the rubric design.

---

## 1. Contemporary philosophy of education on measurement

**Gert Biesta (2009/2010), "Good Education in an Age of Measurement: On the Need to
Reconnect with the Question of Purpose in Education," *Educational Assessment,
Evaluation and Accountability* / Routledge book.**
https://www.researchgate.net/publication/41529875_Good_Education_in_an_Age_of_Measurement_On_the_Need_to_Reconnect_with_the_Question_of_Purpose_in_Education
- Claim: Biesta names "learnification" — the collapse of education's purposes (qualification,
  socialisation, subjectification) into a single measurable "learning outcome" — and asks
  bluntly "are we measuring what we value, or are we just measuring what we can easily
  measure, and thus valuing what we (can) measure?"
- Implication: a rubric that reduces graduation to a points total answers Biesta's question
  the wrong way; the formula should be visibly subordinate to a purpose statement the
  supervisors can defend in words, not just in arithmetic.

**Biesta (2013), *The Beautiful Risk of Education*, Paradigm/Routledge.**
- Claim: good education is inherently risky and cannot be made "strong" (control-proof)
  without also making it weak (meaningless) — teaching and judging both require exposure
  to the possibility of being wrong.
- Implication: a "written justification to deviate from the formula" is not a loophole to be
  minimised but the place where the real educational risk-taking (and hence real judgement)
  happens; it should be normal, not exceptional, practice.

**Jerry Z. Muller (2018), *The Tyranny of Metrics*, Princeton UP.**
https://towardsdatascience.com/on-the-tyranny-of-metrics-and-metric-fixation-b4c1d44b5f6c/
- Claim: "metric fixation" — the belief that replacing professional judgement with
  standardised numerical indicators improves performance — routinely backfires by crowding
  out the judgement it was meant to discipline.
- Implication: hidden component scores that are then summed by formula are exactly Muller's
  target case; the rubric should treat the numbers as an aid to a conversation among
  supervisors, not a self-executing verdict.

**Wendy Espeland & Michael Sauder (2016), *Engines of Anxiety: Academic Rankings,
Reputation, and Accountability*, Russell Sage Foundation.**
https://academic.oup.com/ser/article/16/1/207/4880471
- Claim: commensuration (turning qualitatively different things into one comparable number)
  is "useful," but "useful is not the same as good" — it forecloses the "messier," more
  time-consuming, deliberative and consensus-based modes of evaluation it replaces.
- Implication: an explicit warning belongs in the rubric's preamble — the formula produces a
  number that is comparable, not necessarily a number that is true; deliberation among
  supervisors is where truth is checked.

**Marilyn Strathern (1997), "'Improving ratings': audit in the British university system,"
*European Review* 5(3); popularised as "Strathern's law" via Goodhart's law and Campbell's
law.** Michael Power (1997), *The Audit Society*, Oxford UP.
https://xahteiwi.eu/blog/2021/11/14/meaningless-metrics-treacherous-targets/
- Claim: "when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure" (Strathern,
  building on Goodhart); Power shows how audit, meant to verify quality, becomes a
  self-referential "ritual of verification" detached from the quality it certifies.
- Implication: if a numeric threshold on the rubric becomes the thing students and
  supervisors optimise for, the rubric has stopped measuring the thesis and started
  measuring compliance with the rubric — the deviation-justification clause exists to break
  that loop.

**Martha Nussbaum (2010), *Not for Profit: Why Democracy Needs the Humanities*, Princeton
UP; Stefan Collini (2017), *Speaking of Universities*, Verso.**
https://www.researchgate.net/publication/263369824_Review_of_Martha_Nussbaum_Not_for_Profit_Why_Democracy_Needs_the_Humanities_Princeton_University_Press_2010
- Claim: Nussbaum argues profit/market logic has displaced "imaginative and critical
  self-development" as education's aim; Collini shows audit culture "must always substitute
  things that can be counted for more important things that cannot, while claiming
  nevertheless to care about and measure these more important things."
- Implication: naming, in the rubric text itself, what the formula cannot capture (originality,
  intellectual courage, design judgement) is the honest move Collini says audit culture
  usually skips.

## 2. Judgement and practical wisdom

**Aristotle, *Nicomachean Ethics*, Book VI, on *phronesis* (practical wisdom).**
- Claim: phronesis is the capacity to perceive and act well in a particular, non-repeatable
  situation; unlike *episteme* (theoretical knowledge) or *techne* (rule-governed craft), it
  cannot be reduced to a formula because its object is always the concrete particular.
- Implication: grading a graduation project is a phronesis-type act (this student, this
  project, this moment), not a techne-type act (apply the rule); a rubric can support
  phronesis but cannot replace it.

**Joseph Dunne (1993), *Back to the Rough Ground: "Phronesis" and "Techne" in Modern
Philosophy and in Aristotle*, Notre Dame UP.**
https://read.dukeupress.edu/common-knowledge/article/25/1-3/422/140048/Back-to-the-Rough-Ground-Phronesis-and-Techne-in
- Claim: Dunne traces how modern professions (including teaching) keep collapsing phronesis
  into techne — mistaking the "lure of technique" (procedures, checklists, rubrics) for the
  practical wisdom those tools were meant to serve.
- Implication: the rubric's formula is a techne aid; the named "trusted expert" judgement
  step is where phronesis is explicitly reinstated as senior to the technique.

**Hans-Georg Gadamer, *Truth and Method* (1960; Eng. 1975), on *Urteilskraft* (judgement),
taste, *sensus communis*, and *Bildung*.**
https://transitionconsciousness.wordpress.com/2013/03/09/exploring-gadamers-truth-and-method-the-truth-and-experience-of-art/
- Claim: Gadamer revives the humanist concepts of *sensus communis* (a shared sense
  cultivated by living in a community of judges) and *Bildung* (formation of a person, not
  accumulation of facts) as the proper ground of judgement in the human sciences, against
  method's claim to certainty.
- Implication: "calibration among peers" is literally Gadamer's *sensus communis* in
  institutional form — supervisors' judgement is trustworthy because it is cultivated and
  checked in a community, not because it follows a procedure.

**Immanuel Kant, *Critique of the Power of Judgment* (1790), §§18–22, on aesthetic
judgement as "exemplary" rather than "determinate."**
https://www.gutenberg.org/files/48433/48433-h/48433-h.htm
- Claim: Kant argues aesthetic judgement follows no rule that could be stated in advance
  (it is not "determinate," subsuming a case under a known concept) yet still claims
  universal assent, because it appeals to a presupposed *sensus communis* and offers itself
  as exemplary for others to test their own judgement against.
- Implication: this is the clean philosophical argument for why a formula cannot fully
  specify quality in advance — the closest available substitute for a rule is a track record
  of exemplary, defensible individual judgements that other examiners can calibrate against.

**Michael Polanyi, *Personal Knowledge* (1958) and *The Tacit Dimension* (1966); Elliot
Eisner (1985/1998), *The Enlightened Eye: Qualitative Inquiry and the Enhancement of
Educational Practice*.**
https://infed.org/dir/welcome/michael-polanyi-and-tacit-knowledge/
- Claim: Polanyi — "we can know more than we can tell" — argues connoisseurship is
  transmitted example-to-example, master to apprentice, not by specifiable rule; Eisner
  builds "educational connoisseurship and criticism" directly on this, proposing expert,
  articulate perception (like an art critic's) as a legitimate model of assessment.
- Implication: supervisor expertise built through years of reading theses is a real,
  non-reducible asset (tacit knowledge); the rubric's job is to make that expert perception
  *articulate* (Eisner's "criticism") in the written justification, not to replace it.

## 3. Renaissance humanist pedagogy on evaluating students

**Battista Guarino, *De ordine docendi et studendi* (1459)**, tr. W. H. Woodward, in
*Vittorino da Feltre and Other Humanist Educators*.
https://history.hanover.edu/texts/guarino.html
- Claim: written at his father Guarino Veronese's school at Ferrara, the treatise sets out a
  method (order of authors, memorisation, imitation) but explicitly relies on the teacher's
  continuous personal observation of each pupil's temperament and progress, including
  competitive pairing to spur judgement by comparison.
- Implication: even the founding humanist "curriculum" document treats assessment as
  ongoing expert observation of an individual, not a terminal score.

**Anthony Grafton & Lisa Jardine (1982), "Humanism and the School of Guarino: A Problem of
Evaluation," *Past & Present* 96; expanded in Grafton & Jardine (1986), *From Humanism to
the Humanities: Education and the Liberal Arts in Fifteenth- and Sixteenth-Century Europe*,
Harvard UP.**
https://www.academia.edu/37504809/Anthony_Grafton_and_Lisa_Jardine_Humanism_and_the_School_of_Guarino_A_Problem_of_Evaluation_Past_and_Present_vol_96_no_1_August_1982_51_80
- Claim: their title is itself the counter-argument — Grafton and Jardine argue that
  humanist schooling, for all its rhetoric of cultivating *iudicium* and virtue, could in
  practice decay into rote grammatical drill and formulaic imitation, evaluated by rule-bound
  correctness rather than genuine judgement.
- Implication: an honest rubric page should cite this as the historical warning: naming
  "judgement" as the ideal does not guarantee it is practised — the same slide toward
  proceduralism can happen to a humanist-inspired rubric as happened to humanist schools.

**Erasmus, *De ratione studii* (1511) and *De pueris instituendis* (written earlier, publ.
1529); Juan Luis Vives, *De tradendis disciplinis* (1531), Book II ("De tradendis
disciplinis" proper), tr. Foster Watson as *Vives: On Education*.**
https://philpapers.org/rec/WATVOE
- Claim: Vives gives explicit advice that a teacher should know each pupil individually
  (ingenium, memory, temperament) before judging work, and recommends that teachers confer
  with one another about pupils rather than grade in isolation — an early argument for
  calibration among peers.
- Implication: "written justification to deviate, checked against peers" is a direct
  structural echo of Vives's recommendation that teachers compare notes rather than judge
  alone.

**Baldassare Castiglione, *Il Cortegiano* (1528), on *sprezzatura*; Michel de Montaigne,
"Of the Education of Children" (*Essais* I.26, 1580), "a well-made head is of more worth
than a well-filled one."**
https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sprezzatura
- Claim: Castiglione's ideal courtier displays effortless, un-rule-bound mastery
  (*sprezzatura*) that a rule-follower could never fake; Montaigne explicitly prefers
  cultivated judgement ("une tête bien faite") over the mere accumulation of facts
  ("une tête bien pleine"), and locates the training of judgement in exposure to real
  situations, not formulas.
- Implication: both give the rubric a vocabulary for what a formula structurally cannot
  score — the visible, examinable quality of a graduate's judgement itself, as distinct from
  correct execution of a method.

**The disputatio and the descent of the thesis defence.** Overview: Marijke van der Wal et
al., "The medieval culture of disputation," and general histories of the *disputatio pro
gradu*.
https://www.researchgate.net/publication/289853361_The_medieval_culture_of_disputation_Pedagogy_practice_and_performance
- Claim: the modern public thesis defence descends directly from the medieval and
  Renaissance university *disputatio* — an oral, public, adversarial-but-collegial
  examination in which a candidate defended theses against examiners in real time, judged by
  senior scholars present in the room.
- Implication: TU Delft's graduation defence already *is* the humanist form of examination;
  the rubric's formula is the modern addition, and should be built to support the defence
  as the primary evidentiary event, not to substitute for it.

## 4. *Ingenium* and *iudicium* in humanist rhetoric; *decorum* as proto-desirability

**Quintilian, *Institutio Oratoria*, Book II (esp. II.8, II.9), on assessing pupils' natural
talent (*ingenium*) and judgement (*iudicium*).**
https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Quint.+Inst.+2.1&lang=original
- Claim: Quintilian instructs the teacher to act like a gymnastics master who "expertly
  observes the body and mind" of each pupil, discerning individual *ingenium* rather than
  applying one standard, and specifically warns that raw *ingenium* unaccompanied by
  *iudicium* (judgement) is a poor sign — the two must be assessed together, not one as a
  proxy for the other.
- Implication: a rubric that scores craft/execution (a proxy for *ingenium* + effort) without
  a distinct place for *iudicium* — did the student know why they made this choice, and know
  its limits — is assessing only half of what humanist rhetoric considered gradable.

**Cicero, *De oratore* (55 BCE), on *decorum* — the orator's judgement of what is fitting to
occasion, audience, and purpose.**
- Claim: for Cicero, *decorum* cannot be taught as a rule; it is the orator's trained sense
  of appropriateness — the right register, argument, and structure *for this case* — making
  it arguably the classical ancestor of "fit for purpose" or "desirability" in design
  judgement.
- Implication: a graduation project rubric could explicitly name *decorum*/fit-for-purpose as
  a judged quality distinct from technical correctness — did the student's choices fit their
  own stated problem and audience — which is inherently a supervisor call, not a scored
  checklist item.

## 5. Design as a humanist discipline, and the designer's judgement

**Richard Buchanan (1992), "Wicked Problems in Design Thinking," *Design Issues* 8(2);
Buchanan (2001), "Design Research and the New Learning," *Design Issues* 17(4).**
https://web.mit.edu/jrankin/www/engin_as_lib_art/Design_thinking.pdf
- Claim: Buchanan explicitly names design "the new liberal art of technological culture" and
  argues design problems are "wicked" — indeterminate, without a definitive formulation or
  stopping rule — which is precisely why design work cannot be graded the way a determinate
  engineering problem can.
- Implication: if the MSc project is a design project, the rubric should acknowledge Buchanan's
  point structurally — wicked problems don't have a scoring formula with a defined optimum,
  only better and worse *arguments* for a chosen resolution, which supervisors must judge.

**Harold Nelson & Erik Stolterman (2003/2012), *The Design Way: Intentional Change in an
Unpredictable World*, MIT Press.**
https://csl4d.wordpress.com/2018/09/29/design-judgment-and-the-particular/
- Claim: Nelson and Stolterman make "instrumental judgment" — knowing when, how and why to
  use or adapt a method, not just how to execute it — a core design competence in its own
  right, irreducible to following the method correctly.
- Implication: a strong graduation project should be able to show *where and why* the
  student deviated from a standard method; the rubric's own deviation-justification clause
  mirrors exactly the competence Nelson and Stolterman want examined in the student.

**Klaus Krippendorff (2006), *The Semantic Turn: A New Foundation for Design*, CRC Press.**
https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/17493460600844157
- Claim: Krippendorff argues design's foundation is not technical function but meaning —
  human-centred design must make sense to the range of stakeholders who use, live with, or
  are affected by an artifact, a criterion that is interpretive and stakeholder-relative, not
  a fixed metric.
- Implication: outcomes like "does this make sense to its intended users" resist
  formula-scoring by definition; a rubric can require evidence be presented, but only expert
  supervisors (ideally with some stakeholder proximity) can judge whether the case is made.

## 6. Honest counterpoint: the case for metrics and rules against arbitrary judgement

**D. Royce Sadler (2009), "Indeterminacy in the Use of Preset Criteria for Assessment and
Grading," *Assessment & Evaluation in Higher Education* 34(2); Sadler on "grade integrity."**
https://scispace.com/pdf/grade-integrity-and-the-representation-of-academic-3glntlzkp8.pdf
- Claim: Sadler himself — a foundational voice for criteria-based grading — later documents
  that even preset criteria/rubrics cannot fully specify quality in advance, but he also
  shows that unstructured holistic judgement is measurably unreliable: the same assessor
  regrades the same work differently on different occasions, and halo effects (prior
  impressions of the student) bias the score.
- Implication: the honest reading of Sadler is not "rubrics vs judgement" but "judgement
  needs scaffolding to be reliable" — which supports keeping a formula as a calibration
  check and audit trail on supervisor judgement, not as its replacement.

**Bias in holistic/discretionary evaluation — general literature on the fairness case for
rules.** (See also Espeland & Sauder 2016, above, on why institutions adopt commensurable
metrics in the first place.)
- Claim: the standard equity argument — traceable to Enlightenment arguments for the rule of
  law over arbitrary discretion, and echoed throughout bias research — is that unstructured
  "expert" judgement is exactly where race, gender, class, and personal favour enter
  assessment unchecked, and that explicit criteria constrain (without eliminating) that risk.
- Implication: the rubric's formula and hidden scores are legitimate as a bias check and a
  paper trail — the design problem is not to discard them but to keep the supervisor free to
  override them, in writing, and to have that override itself checked by peers (the very
  mechanism humanist *sensus communis* recommends) so that discretion is disciplined rather
  than unaccountable.

## Notes on use

- Several searches (Vergerio's *De ingenuis moribus*, Bruni's specific pedagogical texts,
  and Cicero's *De oratore* on *decorum* directly) returned general/secondary material
  rather than a directly quotable primary passage online; the claims above for those are
  drawn from standard intellectual-history knowledge of the texts and should be checked
  against a library copy (e.g. Craig Kallendorf's Loeb/ITRL edition of *Humanist Educational
  Treatises*, which contains Vergerio, Bruni, and Guarino together) before quoting verbatim
  on the rubric page.
- The Grafton & Jardine "Problem of Evaluation" piece (§3) is the single most directly
  on-topic academic source found — it is literally a historical case study of humanist
  claims about judgement failing to survive contact with institutional pedagogy, which is
  exactly the risk this rubric project is trying to avoid repeating.
