# Calibrating assessors vs. points-and-formula rubrics: evidence review

Prepared 2026-09-18 for the TU Delft MSc graduation-project rubric redesign. Question: does the evidence support the supervisor's position — that the fix for grading inconsistency is **aligning assessors on what "excellent" means, plus written justification**, rather than (or in addition to) a more elaborate points-formula rubric?

Short answer: yes, strongly. The measurement-science and higher-ed-assessment literatures converge on the same conclusion — rubrics with fixed criteria and weights give an *illusion* of objectivity while the real variance sits in assessors' internal standards, which only shared, socially-negotiated exposure to exemplars (calibration/moderation/comparative judgement) can close. Written justification is the accountability layer that makes calibrated judgement defensible on appeal. None of this eliminates a rubric outright — the strongest designs pair a light rubric (to structure the write-up and channel dialogue) with a genuine calibration process.

---

## 1. Sadler: calibration as the alternative to codification

- **Sadler, D.R. (2013).** "Assuring academic achievement standards: from moderation to calibration." *Assessment in Education: Principles, Policy & Practice*, 20(1), 5–19. DOI: [10.1080/0969594X.2012.714742](https://doi.org/10.1080/0969594X.2012.714742)
  **Claim:** Written criteria and standards can never fully specify what "excellent" looks like, because the descriptors themselves require interpretation — so consistency has to live in assessors' *calibrated* internal reference standards, built through repeated, compared exposure to real work, not in better wording of the rubric.
  **Implication for TU Delft:** No amount of rewriting the rubric's 9/10 descriptor will fix inconsistent grading by itself; the fix is a recurring process that lets graduation committees compare judgements against shared exemplars until a "9" means the same thing across supervisors.

- **Sadler, D.R. (2010).** "Fidelity as a precondition for integrity in grading academic achievement." *Assessment & Evaluation in Higher Education*, 35(6), 727–743. DOI: [10.1080/02602930902977756](https://doi.org/10.1080/02602930902977756)
  **Claim:** Grades lose integrity when non-achievement elements (effort, improvement, compliance, personal rapport with the supervisor) get folded into the number, because they contaminate the signal the grade is supposed to carry.
  **Implication:** A justification requirement should explicitly force assessors to name which parts of the argument are pure achievement-evidence, guarding against exactly this contamination in MSc thesis grading (e.g., crediting effort or a difficult personal year rather than the work's quality).

- **O'Donovan, B., Sadler, I., & Reimann, N. (2024).** "Social moderation and calibration versus codification: a way forward for academic standards in higher education?" *Studies in Higher Education*, 49(12), 2693–2706. DOI: [10.1080/03075079.2024.2321504](https://doi.org/10.1080/03075079.2024.2321504)
  **Claim:** Directly pits the two paradigms (codify standards ever more precisely vs. calibrate people through social moderation) and concludes codification alone has empirically failed to raise reliability, while calibration approaches show more promise but need sustained institutional investment to survive staff turnover.
  **Implication:** This is close to a direct answer to the supervisor's framing — worth reading in full as the most current synthesis before finalizing the rubric page.

## 2. Consensus / social moderation

- **Adie, L.E., Lloyd, M., & Beutel, D. (2013).** "Identifying discourses of moderation in higher education." *Assessment & Evaluation in Higher Education*, 38(8), 968–977. DOI: [10.1080/02602938.2013.769200](https://doi.org/10.1080/02602938.2013.769200)
  **Claim:** Moderation functions through (at least) four different institutional discourses — equity, justification, community-building, accountability — that are often conflated, so a moderation process designed for one purpose (e.g. compliance) will not deliver the benefits of another (e.g. shared standards).
  **Implication:** TU Delft should be explicit about which purpose the calibration sessions serve (building shared standards among supervisors, not just paperwork compliance for the exam board) or the sessions will be run for the wrong goal.

- **Bloxham, S., Hughes, C., & Adie, L. (2016).** "What's the point of moderation? A discussion of the purposes achieved through contemporary moderation practices." *Assessment & Evaluation in Higher Education*, 41(4), 638–653.
  **Claim:** Moderation as commonly practiced (post-hoc sampling and sign-off) mostly protects institutions rather than building genuinely shared standards; the practices that actually change assessor judgement are pre-marking calibration and dialogic discussion of borderline cases, not after-the-fact checking.
  **Implication:** A rubric-plus-checklist "moderation" step bolted onto the current process will satisfy quality assurance but not solve the "what is a 9" problem — the calibration has to happen *before* grading, not as a sign-off after.

- **Watty, K., Freeman, M., Howieson, B., Hancock, P., O'Connell, B., de Lange, P., & Abraham, A. (2014).** "Achievement matters: external peer review of accounting learning standards." *Higher Education Research & Development*, 33(1), 125–138. Full project report: Office for Learning and Teaching (Australia), 2015.
  **Claim:** A national external-calibration exercise (35 academics, 17 institutions) using shared exemplar scripts measurably improved cross-institution agreement on where student work sat against learning standards, and the calibration step itself — not the standards documents — was what participants valued and what produced the improvement.
  **Implication:** Even a small-scale internal version (a handful of TU Delft graduation committees calibrating against 3–5 shared exemplar theses) is the mechanism with direct empirical support, more than refining the written standard.

- **Krause, K.L., Scott, G., Aubin, K., et al. (2014).** *Assuring Learning and Teaching Standards through Inter-Institutional Peer Review and Moderation.* Final report, Office for Learning and Teaching, Australia.
  **Claim:** A sector-wide inter-university moderation model for final-year/capstone work is feasible and increases confidence in comparability of standards, but requires structured calibration meetings with explicit exemplars, not just exchanged criteria sheets.
  **Implication:** Confirms the "meeting + exemplars" design pattern generalizes to capstone/thesis-level work specifically (closest analogue to an MSc graduation project among these sources).

## 3. Exemplars and benchmark portfolios

- **Carless, D., & Chan, K.K.H. (2017).** "Managing dialogic use of exemplars." *Assessment & Evaluation in Higher Education*, 42(6), 930–941. DOI: [10.1080/02602938.2016.1211246](https://doi.org/10.1080/02602938.2016.1211246)
  **Claim:** Exemplars only build shared standards when used *dialogically* — students/assessors discuss and justify judgements about them rather than simply reading them as models to imitate; teacher-withheld evaluative judgement during the discussion is what produces genuine calibration rather than copying.
  **Implication:** A calibration session should be run as a facilitated discussion where supervisors argue out loud about a shared exemplar thesis's grade before comparing to a reference score — not a one-way circulation of "here are some 9s."

- **Handley, K., & Williams, L. (2011).** "From copying to learning: using exemplars to engage students with assessment criteria and feedback." *Assessment & Evaluation in Higher Education*, 36(1), 95–108.
  **Claim:** Exemplars improve students' grasp of standards only when paired with active interpretation tasks; passive distribution of "model answers" has weak or even counterproductive effects (students copy surface features).
  **Implication:** Any bank of benchmark graduation-project write-ups TU Delft builds should come with guided annotation/discussion prompts for supervisors, not just be archived as reference PDFs.

## 4. Comparative judgement as an alternative to absolute scoring

- **Pollitt, A. (2012).** "The method of Adaptive Comparative Judgement." *Assessment in Education: Principles, Policy & Practice*, 19(3), 281–300. DOI: [10.1080/0969594X.2012.665354](https://doi.org/10.1080/0969594X.2012.665354)
  **Claim:** Asking assessors only for relative, holistic judgements ("which of these two is better") rather than absolute scores against criteria produces markedly higher measured reliability than conventional marking, because humans are much better at comparison than at absolute calibration to a numeric scale.
  **Implication:** For borderline/9-and-above cases specifically, a paired comparison against 2–3 known-excellent past theses may pin down "is this really a 9" more reliably than filling in a rubric's point boxes.

- **Verhavert, S., Bouwer, R., Donche, V., & De Maeyer, S. (2019).** "A meta-analysis on the reliability of comparative judgement." *Assessment in Education: Principles, Policy & Practice*, 26(5), 541–562. DOI: [10.1080/0969594X.2019.1602027](https://doi.org/10.1080/0969594X.2019.1602027)
  **Claim:** Across 49 CJ assessments, reliability (~.70) is reached with roughly 10–14 comparisons per piece of work, with faster convergence for expert/peer judges than novices.
  **Implication:** Full CJ is likely too costly for every MSc thesis (10+ pairwise judgements per project), but is realistic as a *calibration* device — running committee members through 10–14 comparisons of past benchmark theses once a year to anchor the top of the scale.

- **Jones, I., & Alcock, L. (2014).** "Peer assessment without assessment criteria." *Studies in Higher Education*, 39(10), 1774–1787.
  **Claim:** Comparative judgement without any explicit criteria produced peer-assessment outcomes strongly correlated with expert judgement (r ≈ .77) and with acceptable inter-rater reliability (r ≈ .72), undercutting the assumption that written criteria are necessary for reliable grading.
  **Implication:** Direct evidence against the premise that a more detailed rubric is the primary lever — holistic comparative judgement by calibrated people beat criterion-by-criterion scoring here.

- **Bartholomew, S.R., Strimel, G.J., & Yoshikawa, E. (2019).** "Using adaptive comparative judgment for student formative feedback and learning during a middle school design project." *International Journal of Technology and Design Education*, 29(2), 363–385.
  **Claim:** ACJ produces high inter-rater reliability specifically for open-ended design-project work (the closest analogue in this literature to an engineering/design MSc thesis), where conventional rubric marking struggles because there is no single correct solution.
  **Implication:** Relevant precedent for design-oriented TU Delft faculties (IDE, ME, Architecture) where "quality" is inherently multidimensional and hard to decompose into independent rubric rows.
  *(No published use of CJ found specifically for full MSc/PhD thesis grading — this is a gap; the nearest evidence is design-portfolio and capstone-adjacent work above.)*

## 5. Examiner calibration in practice: UK and Dutch systems

- **QAA / UK Standing Committee for Quality Assessment (UKSCQA).** *External Examining Principles* and *UK Common Principles for Undergraduate Degree Classification.* [qaa.ac.uk/the-quality-code/external-examining-principles](https://www.qaa.ac.uk/the-quality-code/external-examining-principles)
  **Claim:** The ~200-year-old UK external examiner system explicitly requires examiners to take part in discipline-level *calibration activities* and treats shared classification descriptors as a baseline for judgement, not a formula — comparability is achieved via a person moving across institutions and comparing standards, not via identical rubrics.
  **Implication:** Supports building an equivalent light-touch role at TU Delft — e.g., a second/external committee member whose explicit job includes comparing this year's borderline 9s against other programs' benchmarks, rather than only checking rubric-box arithmetic.

- **Hsiao, Y.-P.A., van de Watering, G., Heitbrink, M., Vlas, H., & Chiu, M.-S. (2023/2024).** "Ensuring bachelor's thesis assessment quality: a case study at one Dutch research university." *Higher Education Evaluation and Development*, 18(1), 2–16. DOI: [10.1108/HEED-08-2022-0033](https://doi.org/10.1108/HEED-08-2022-0033)
  **Claim:** At a Dutch research university, examiner training (one day, practicing on real theses with rubrics, discussed with senior examiners), independent double-scoring with mandatory face-to-face moderation on disagreements, and periodic recalibration of borderline cases together improved consistency; the rubric alone, without the training/moderation layer, was reported as insufficient.
  **Implication:** This is the closest direct Dutch precedent to the TU Delft case and gives a concrete implementable design: train new examiners on real past theses, require the two assessors to score independently and then reconcile face-to-face (not just average), and periodically re-run calibration on borderline/high grades specifically.

- University of Twente, *Enhancing the reliability of graduation projects assessment* (Examination Board guidance): describes independent double marking plus a structured moderation conversation as the mechanism used to safeguard graduation-project assessment quality at a comparable Dutch technical university. [utwente.nl — Assessing Graduation projects: Enhancing Reliability](https://www.utwente.nl/en/examination-board/Safeguarding_assessment_quality/graduation-process/Assessing_Graduation_projects_Enhancing_Reliability/)
  **Implication:** A Dutch TU peer institution's official examination-board policy already codifies "independent scoring + moderation conversation," which TU Delft can benchmark against directly rather than reason from first principles.

## 6. Inter-rater reliability of thesis/dissertation grades, and what calibration buys

- **Hand, L., & Clewes, D. (2000).** "Marking the difference: an investigation of the criteria used for assessing undergraduate dissertations in a business school." *Assessment & Evaluation in Higher Education*, 25(1), 5–21.
  **Claim:** Even within one school with a shared marking scheme, markers applied dissertation criteria very differently in practice — the written criteria were consistently reinterpreted according to markers' personal priorities.
  **Implication:** Baseline evidence for the problem TU Delft is trying to solve: a written rubric does not, by itself, produce consistent application even inside a single department.

- **Saunders, M.N.K., & Davis, S.N. (1998).** "The use of assessment criteria to ensure consistency of marking: some implications for good practice." *Quality Assurance in Education*, 6(3), 162–171. DOI: [10.1108/09684889810220465](https://doi.org/10.1108/09684889810220465)
  **Claim:** Explicit assessment criteria reduce but do not eliminate marker disagreement, and much of the remaining variance is attributable to differing weightings markers implicitly give the same criteria.
  **Implication:** Confirms that a "points and formula" fix addresses only part of the variance (weighting is often the real disagreement, not the criteria list) — calibration on *how much each criterion matters* for excellence is the missing piece a static formula can't supply.

- Webster, F., Pepper, D., & Jenkins, A. (2000). "Assessing the undergraduate dissertation." *Assessment & Evaluation in Higher Education*, 25(1), 71–80.
  **Claim:** Criteria for "what counts" in a dissertation vary substantially even across departments in the same institution, let alone across individual assessors.
  **Implication:** Reinforces that if TU Delft's graduation committees span different research groups/chairs, calibration needs to explicitly surface and reconcile cross-group differences in what "excellent" research looks like, not assume a university-wide rubric text will do it.

## 7. Written justification as an accountability mechanism

- **Holbrook, A., Bourke, S., Lovat, T., & Fairbairn, H. (2004/2008).** "Consistency and inconsistency in PhD thesis examination" and related work on PhD examiner reports (804 Australian theses analyzed). *Studies in Higher Education* / other venues.
  **Claim:** Examiners' written reports are where the real evaluative reasoning surfaces — a mixed-methods analysis of 804 reports found the great majority of written justifications were internally consistent with each other and with the final decision, with inconsistency concentrated in a small minority of cases, and that report quality (specificity, evidence-referencing) is what makes a grade defensible on scrutiny/appeal.
  **Implication:** A mandatory, structured written justification (tied to specific evidence in the thesis, not just a score) is itself an evidence-backed mechanism for catching the rare miscalibrated grade before it goes final, and for making a 9-or-above defensible if challenged — directly supporting the supervisor's proposal.

- **Ginsburg, S., et al. (2013, 2015, 2017).** "The hidden value of narrative comments for assessment: a quantitative reliability analysis of qualitative data" (*Academic Medicine*, 92(11), 1617–1621) and related papers on narrative evaluation in medical education.
  **Claim:** Narrative/written comments, properly analyzed, can discriminate between performance levels as reliably as numeric ratings, and raters/readers can consistently decode an implicit "hidden curriculum" of evaluative language — narrative is not inherently less reliable than numbers, contrary to common assumption.
  **Implication:** Directly rebuts the implicit worry that moving weight from a numeric rubric formula toward written justification sacrifices reliability; the evidence says well-structured narrative assessment can match or exceed numeric-only scoring for capturing genuine quality differences.

## Synthesis: what a calibration-first design would look like, and its cost vs. double-scoring a rubric

**Design implied by the evidence** (combining Sadler, Watty et al., Hsiao et al., Carless & Chan, and the CJ literature):

1. **Keep a light rubric** — a short set of dimensions (e.g., problem framing, methodological rigor, originality, communication) used to *structure* the graduation report and the assessors' written justification, not to auto-compute a grade by formula. Sadler and Saunders & Davis both show that criteria lists reduce but don't eliminate disagreement, and that weighting is where the real variance hides — so a formula that hard-codes weights simply hides the disagreement rather than resolving it.
2. **Run an annual calibration session per program/committee cluster**, using 3–5 real past theses spanning borderline-pass through genuinely excellent, discussed dialogically (Carless & Chan) — ideally with a comparative-judgement pass (rank the exemplars, then compare to the historical consensus grade) to anchor what a 9/10 actually looks like, per Pollitt/Verhavert's evidence that 10–14 comparisons stabilize judgement even for a small panel.
3. **Require independent double assessment with a mandatory face-to-face reconciliation meeting** (not an averaged score) whenever the two assessors' grades diverge, or whenever the grade is ≥9 — following the Dutch (Hsiao et al.) and UT Twente precedents directly.
4. **Require a structured written justification** tied to specific evidence in the thesis for every grade, especially ≥9 — modeled on the PhD-examiner-report evidence (Holbrook & Bourke) and narrative-assessment evidence (Ginsburg), both of which show written reasoning is a genuinely reliable, appeal-defensible signal, not a weaker cousin of the number.
5. **Periodically re-calibrate**, because Sadler's and O'Donovan et al.'s core point is that calibration decays — new supervisors and drifting norms mean this can't be a one-time rollout; UK external examiners institutionalize this via rotating external moderators, which TU Delft's Board of Examiners role could formalize further.

**Cost, per the evidence, compared with double-scoring a static rubric:**
- Double-scoring against a fixed rubric is *not* actually cheaper than calibration in the studies that measured it — Hsiao et al.'s Dutch case still required double marking *plus* a moderation meeting *plus* annual training to get acceptable consistency; double-scoring alone (two people filling in the same rubric independently, no reconciliation) is the condition the literature repeatedly shows still leaves large disagreement (Hand & Clewes; Saunders & Davis).
- The calibration literature's own cost figures are modest and front-loaded: Watty et al.'s national exercise ran as a single calibration workshop per cycle; Hsiao et al.'s Dutch training was one day per new examiner per year; Verhavert et al.'s CJ reliability threshold (10–14 comparisons) is achievable in a single 2–3 hour session for a handful of benchmark cases, not for every thesis.
- The marginal cost calibration adds beyond ordinary double-marking is essentially **one shared session (a few hours, once or twice a year) plus a reconciliation conversation on divergent/borderline cases** — small relative to the reliability gain reported, and it is a one-time-per-cohort calibration cost rather than a per-thesis cost, unlike a heavier points-and-formula scheme that still requires per-thesis double-scoring effort without buying the same reliability.
- **Net:** the evidence favors calibration-plus-justification as *more cost-effective per unit of reliability* than trying to engineer an ever-more-detailed points formula, because the formula approach keeps paying the double-scoring cost every thesis while never actually removing the standard-drift problem that only shared exposure to exemplars fixes.
